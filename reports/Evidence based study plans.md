# Schedule spaced retrieval, not study time

The research says a good university study plan is a queue of retrieval sessions, not a calendar of topics: each lecture's concepts are recalled with feedback on lecture day and again on three or more later days, with gaps that widen as the exam gets further away. That protocol, successive relearning, is the only complete study schedule shown to raise real university exam scores, by **about 10 percentage points**, with recall a month later of **over 60% against under 20%**. Retrieval practice (g ≈ 0.5 across 222 classroom studies) and spacing are strong evidence; interleaving, pretesting, mock exams and sleep regularity are real but narrower than usually claimed; the two-day buffer, the light exam day, "review within 24 hours", Pomodoro, the two-hours-per-credit norm and every exam-date trick for FSRS are convention or untested. The behavioural evidence is harsher: reminders, planning prompts and dashboards produce precise nulls on grades at scale, while plans sized to measured pace, countable task goals and limited built-in slack have support. Slap already owns every ingredient (transcripts, concepts with mastery, FSRS cards, questions, recall check, pretest, exam dates), but its plan schedules almost none of them: the model-written plan is free text that cannot see study history, practice questions have no spacing or criterion, FSRS ignores the exam and releases every new card at once, quizzes are treated as exams, and nothing records whether a day was done. The build order that follows is: make a deterministic scheduler the source of truth and reduce the model to writing captions; add a relearning ladder per lecture (day 0, +2, +5, +9, then gaps sized to exam distance); give the plan a time budget from logged pace and forward re-planning after missed days; then add exam-aware card passes and a mock exam. No controlled study of exam-aware scheduling exists, so Slap's own quiz and exam dates are the best available instrument for finding out which of these rules earns its place.

## Four techniques carry the evidence, and three of them are one loop

**Retrieval practice is the best-supported study activity and the classroom effect is about half a standard deviation.** The largest classroom meta-analysis (222 studies, 48,478 students) puts quizzing at g = 0.499 overall, 0.427 after bias adjustment, **0.486 in university courses** and 0.33 when the comparison is restudying the same material ([Yang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309)); the laboratory estimate against restudy is g = 0.50 ([Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)). Four moderators matter for a planner. Corrective feedback lifts the classroom effect from 0.37 to 0.54, and in the lab retrieval without feedback yields nothing when the student recalls half or less (g = 0.03). Repetition helps with diminishing returns: one quiz gives 0.44, two 0.60, three or more 0.64. Matching the practice format to the exam format gives 0.53 against 0.40. And post-class quizzes (0.54) beat pre-class ones (0.19) ([Yang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309); [Rowland 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)). Format evidence conflicts between lab and classroom, but answer-production with feedback is the safe default: short answer reaches 0.64 in classrooms, multiple choice 0.57, and unprompted free recall is the weakest classroom format (0.24) yet the strongest lab format once feedback is guaranteed (0.81). The benefit is also specific: tested content gains 0.51, untested content from the same course 0.32, and fact quizzes do not improve higher-order performance while higher-order quizzes do ([Yang et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309); [Agarwal 2019, summarised](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/27-1)).

One finding should shape how the app reports progress. In the classic prose experiments, rereading won on a test five minutes later (81% against 75%) and lost a week later (42% against 56%); in the follow-up, students who studied the passage four times predicted they would remember most and then forgot 52% in a week, against 14% for the group that studied once and tested three times ([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)). **Same-session performance is not a valid mastery signal**, and students will feel the better method working worse.

**Spacing is equally strong in direction, but its parameters come from one study of trivia facts.** With study time held constant, 1,354 adults who restudied 32 facts after the best gap recalled 10% more than a zero-gap group at a 7-day test, 59% more at 35 days and 111% more at 70 days; the optimal gap grew from about 3 days for a test a week away to 8 days at five weeks and 12 days at ten weeks, shrinking as a share of the delay from 43% to 17% ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)). Two properties matter more than the exact numbers. The cost is asymmetric, since accuracy "increases steeply and then declines much more gradually" as the gap grows, so a scheduler in doubt should round gaps up. And spacing barely matters at a one-week horizon while roughly doubling recall at ten weeks, so spacing rules earn their cost for midterms and cumulative finals, not weekly quizzes. The one lecture-based test agrees: undergraduates who reviewed a simulated lecture after 8 days outscored those who reviewed after 1 day on both factual and application questions five weeks later ([Kapler et al. 2015](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/KWW2015.pdf)). The popular belief that intervals must expand has no support; a meta-analysis of 29 studies found spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval by g = 0.74 with no reliable difference between expanding and uniform schedules ([Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). No parametric study exists for three or more sessions on real course material, so every multi-session schedule, including the one recommended below, is extrapolation.

**Successive relearning is those two techniques run as a loop, and it is the protocol with exam scores behind it.** The student retrieves each item with feedback until it is recalled correctly, drops it for that session, and repeats the whole set on later days. In an introductory psychology course, students began a stack of 6 to 8 concepts when the lecturer introduced them and relearned it in three or more sessions at least a day apart; exam performance on those concepts was more than 10% higher than on concepts studied their own way, on application questions, and 24 days after the exam they recalled over 60% against under 20% ([Dunlosky et al. 2023, describing Rawson et al. 2013](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)). A biopsychology course replicated the roughly 10% boost ([Janes et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3699)), and a time-matched comparison against restudy on three separate days after each lecture found better recall 42 and 68 days later ([Higham et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000693)). The authors supply planner-ready numbers: 8 to 10 definitions per set; 30 to 40 minutes for the first session, about 15 for the second and under 5 after that; "three extra sessions separated by 2 days"; two or three relearning sessions inside a four-week exam cycle; one or two boosters before a cumulative final ([Dunlosky et al. 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)). Later sessions carry the benefit: a higher first-session criterion stops mattering once relearning sessions follow, so time is better spent on another spaced session than on drilling to three correct in the first ([Vaughn et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0606-y)). The limits are real. All three course studies are in psychology; the gain on novel probability problems was small (d = 0.28); Higham found the benefit "minimal for transfer and new questions"; and the method fails when students score themselves leniently or decide for themselves when to drop an item ([Dunlosky et al. 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)). In lab work, 58% of dropped flashcards were dropped after a single correct recall, at an average confidence of only 51% ([Kornell & Bjork 2007](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf)).

**Interleaving is the fourth technique and the most conditional.** Its average effect is g = 0.42, or 0.29 after correcting for publication bias, but that hides a split: strong for telling similar categories apart, 0.34 for mathematics problems, null for expository text and negative for word lists (g = −0.39). It also works only when the mixed items sit back to back (0.73) and not when they are spread out (0.22) ([Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)). The classroom wins are in problem-solving courses where the student must first identify the problem type: a university physics course saw median gains of 50% and 125% on surprise tests ([Samani & Pan 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-021-00110-x)). For a transcript-driven app whose items are mostly concept definitions, the supported use is narrow: mix practice questions from the current lecture with earlier ones inside a single set, and contrast confusable concepts directly. Putting different topics on different days is spacing, not interleaving.

Two supporting findings round this out. Self-explanation and "why is this true" prompts have medium effects (g = 0.55) and address the weak transfer of definition recall ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/34322)). Rereading, highlighting and summarising are rated low utility ([Dunlosky 2013](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/dunlosky_0.pdf)), and in one biology course re-watching lectures and rewriting notes were negatively associated with exam scores while completing old exams, problem sets, self-quizzing and explaining concepts each predicted 4 to 8 more points ([Walck-Shannon et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8108503/)). The honest overall claim to make to students is a third to half a standard deviation on the content they practise. Across all ten well-known techniques, effects on deep outcomes (d = 0.26) are less than half those on surface outcomes (0.60) ([Donoghue & Hattie 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216/full)), and whether the testing effect survives for highly interconnected material is still disputed ([van Gog & Sweller 2015](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/not-new-but-nearly-forgotten-the-testing-effect-decreases-or-even/); [Karpicke & Aue 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3)).

## From lecture day to exam day, little of the calendar has been tested

**The first retrieval belongs on lecture day, but "within 24 hours" is a convenience, not a finding.** Every successful course protocol started practice when the lecture introduced the material and continued on separate days ([Dunlosky et al. 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)). No experiment compares a first review at 0, 24 and 48 hours with later sessions held constant, and the "you forget 50 to 80% in a day" figures quoted for lectures trace to no primary lecture study. What the evidence does say is that a same-day pass alone is the worst schedule, the zero-gap condition ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)). Slap's three-minute recall check fits as the opening of session zero precisely because it is followed by got, missed and wrong feedback; without that step free recall is the weakest format.

**Pretesting earns three minutes, aimed at the lecture's own key points.** A preregistered meta-analysis found a moderate benefit for the content the prequestions asked about (g = 0.54, 97 effects) and essentially none for the rest of the lecture (g = 0.04, 91 effects), with factual prequestions (0.58) working better than conceptual ones (0.28); the delay between prequestion and lecture has been manipulated in only two studies, and classroom transfer is flagged by the authors as unproven ([St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)). The implication is to choose prequestions that cover the exam-relevant core and ask the same questions again afterwards, so the narrow effect compounds with retrieval practice.

**Gaps between sessions should scale with distance to the exam.** The table converts the measured optima into a lookup. It is measured for two sessions on simple facts and extrapolated to more; the optimum is a broad plateau and overshooting by up to double costs little, so these are minimums. The horizon is the furthest exam that covers the concept (a cumulative final, where there is one), because that is where spacing pays most.

| Days until the exam that covers the concept | Gap between relearning sessions | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 7 or fewer | 1 to 2 days | Measured: best tested gap 1 day, interpolated optimum 3 ([Cepeda et al. 2008](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)) |
| 8 to 14 | about 4 days | Interpolated between the 7-day and 35-day conditions |
| 15 to 35 | about 7 days | Measured at 35 days (8 days); agrees with [Kapler et al. 2015](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/KWW2015.pdf) |
| 36 to 70 | 10 to 12 days | Measured at 70 days (12 days) |
| More than 70 | 14 to 21 days | Measured at 350 days (27 days) |

**Starting early does not help by itself; what fills the sessions does.** In a 502-student biology course, the day students began studying (mean 5.8 days before the exam) predicted neither that exam, a delayed post-test, nor the cumulative final once total time was controlled, and each extra hour was worth only 0.13 to 0.20 points, whereas each additional active strategy went with 1.9 to 2.8% higher scores ([Walck-Shannon et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8108503/)). Self-reported spacing and cramming were not significantly related to GPA in a 324-student survey, while self-testing was ([Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)). The steelman for cramming is therefore that it does not obviously cost the next exam; its measured cost is what survives afterwards, which becomes an exam-score cost wherever a final is cumulative or a later course builds on this one.

**A timed mock exam is supported; its number and timing are not.** Students who took a voluntary practice test timed to match the exam scored 4 to 7 points higher on each of four exams (three of the four differences significant) and were better calibrated on the first, though the lowest quartile stayed overconfident even after feedback and uptake fell to 55% before the cumulative final ([Osterhage 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8442020/)). These data are observational, but a field experiment with about 4,000 undergraduates estimated a return of 1.49 course points per practice exam from within-student variation ([Clark et al. 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)). No study tests how many mocks or how many days ahead. Placing one three to five days out, so one or two repair sessions remain, is an inference, and because weak students do not self-correct the software should turn every missed item into scheduled work.

**Cumulative review helps weaker students most.** Sections with cumulative exams retained more months later, with the gain concentrated in low scorers ([Lawrence 2013, summarised](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/educational-assessment/examining-the-benefits-of-cumulative-tests-and-finals/)), while a biology study found no average effect and an interaction in which the lowest reasoners gained about 15% and the highest lost about as much ([Bailey et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8062001)). The share of weekly time that should go to old material has never been tested.

**The final days are governed by custom.** "No new material in the last 48 hours", tapering, and Cornell's five-day plan (about 2 hours a day, 10 hours 45 minutes in total, ending with an hour of self-testing) cite no experiments ([Cornell LSC](https://lsc.cornell.edu/how-to-study/studying-for-and-taking-exams/the-five-day-study-plan/)). The nearest real evidence is on sleep, and it points away from the night before: in an MIT chemistry class sleep duration, quality and consistency over the preceding week and month accounted for nearly 25% of variance in grades while the single night before a test showed no relation ([Okano et al. 2019](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/127257)); sleep regularity correlated r = 0.37 with performance at Harvard ([Phillips et al. 2017](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/113615)). The one randomised result is the most useful: a week of 5-hour nights impaired crammed vocabulary and left spaced vocabulary intact ([Huang et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989257)). A spaced schedule is itself the protection against a bad exam week, and a plan should cut scope before it extends into the night.

**Session structure and workload norms are product decisions.** Pre-set breaks of 24/6 or 12/3 minutes reduced fatigue against self-chosen breaks in one day-long study of 87 students ([Biwer et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)), but a follow-up with 94 students found no difference in task completion, productivity or flow between Pomodoro, Flowtime and self-regulated breaks; neither measured learning ([Smits et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)). No study varies session length against learning, and the "four hours a day" ceiling rests on a thinly sourced paragraph ([spot check of Ericsson et al. 1993](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/8EmPhoxgSXGSdq4Cb/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the)). The credit-hour rule of two out-of-class hours per credit is a regulatory definition ([University of Cincinnati](https://uc.edu/about/registrar/registration/policies/credit-hour-definition.html)); US students report about 14 hours a week preparing for all classes ([NSSE 2013](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/items/8866215a-6ea9-4dee-b051-1aa72b413025/full)), which for five courses is roughly 24 to 34 minutes per course per day against the 50 to 70 the norm implies. No study tests how to split time between concurrent courses.

## FSRS has no exam mode, and nobody has tested one

**FSRS-6 is the current deployable version and it schedules for steady retention, not for a date.** Anki 26.09.3 and ts-fsrs both ship FSRS-6 (21 parameters, trainable decay); FSRS-7 exists in the benchmark and is called final by its co-developer, but an Anki forum moderator confirmed in June 2026 that it was not merged ([FSRS algorithm wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm); [Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/when-will-anki-integrate-fsrs-7/69997)). The model schedules each review for the moment predicted recall falls to the desired retention, 0.90 by default, and the manual warns that "above 90% the workload increases very quickly" ([Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html)). The documented exam tools are four: desired retention, a maximum-interval cap, the helper add-on's Advance (which pulls forward the cards proportionally closest to due "before a test"), and filtered decks ([fsrs4anki-helper](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki-helper)). The only developer guidance is a forum post: "Set max. interval in a way that ensures that you will see each card at least once before the exam" and "choose a high-but-not-crazy-high value of desired retention"; stepping retention from 0.90 to 0.95 two weeks out is a user suggestion that its own proposer had not tried ([Anki forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/how-do-i-use-fsrs-for-exam/43631)).

What this means in days is concrete. Running Slap's installed ts-fsrs 5.4.2 with its defaults for this report (a computation, not a published figure), a new card rated Good every time is seen on day 0, day 2 and day 13, and then not until day 59. A concept taught four to eight weeks before an exam therefore gets its last scheduled card review more than a month before it. The course protocols that raised exam scores used a first session on lecture day and three or more relearning sessions on later days, which their authors suggest spacing about two days apart. No study compares the two. FSRS is answering "keep recall near 90% with the fewest reviews", which is a different question from "be as ready as possible on the 14th". Raising retention to 0.95 moves the same card to days 0, 1, 4, 12 and 31, and under the FSRS-6 default curve cuts every interval to about 40% of its length. That is a blunt and expensive way to buy the extra sessions.

**RemNote's Exam Scheduler is the best-documented exam layer, and it leaves the memory model alone.** It adds a learning period (a new card repeats until recalled twice before spacing starts), spreads unseen cards evenly up to the exam, runs "one last review of every card just before your exam", proposes a temporary daily-goal increase when the student is behind instead of silently rescheduling, and computes an exam daily goal from the work remaining ([RemNote: Understanding the Exam Scheduler](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9102040-understanding-the-exam-scheduler); [RemNote: Preparing for an Exam](https://help.remnote.com/en/articles/9101991-preparing-for-an-exam)). It publishes no evaluation. SuperMemo's position sets the lower bound: spaced repetition is "nearly useless for cramming knowledge that is supposed to last less than a week" ([SuperMemo Guru](https://supermemo.guru/wiki/Myth:_SuperMemo_is_a_great_tool_for_cramming)), which matches Cepeda's 10% gain at a seven-day horizon.

**Backlog practice is convention backed by simulation.** Anki defaults to 20 new cards and 200 reviews a day, advises pausing new cards while a backlog exists, and since 24.11 recommends descending retrievability for backlogs because "simulations have shown this is a better choice" ([Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html); [Anki 24.11 release notes](https://newreleases.io/project/github/ankitects/anki/release/24.11)). The helper's Postpone defers the proportionally least overdue cards and stretches their interval by 5 to 10% ([postpone.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki-helper/main/schedule/postpone.py)). The one piece of theory with an experiment behind it is that new-item intake is the control variable: a queueing model of Leitner predicts "a sharp phase transition" in outcomes as the introduction rate rises, supported by a Mechanical Turk test ([Reddy et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.07032)).

**The only exam-score evidence for a scheduling choice is one middle-school study.** Personalised, model-driven review in a semester-long Spanish course yielded a 16.5% boost on a cumulative exam over massed study and 10.0% over one-size-fits-all spacing, at matched review time ([Lindsey et al. 2014](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797613504302/)), and a press summary reports the gain was largest for material from the earliest chapters ([ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140121093046.htm)). Everything else is predictive benchmarking (FSRS-6 beats SM-2 for 99.6% of users, and personal parameters beat defaults for 84.3% ([Expertium benchmark](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html))) or engagement testing. Duolingo's half-life regression halved prediction error yet its first A/B test moved daily activity by 0.3% and cut practice sessions by 7.3% ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174.pdf)). Better memory prediction has never been linked to exam results, and no controlled test exists of a retention ramp, a final review pass or an exam-tied interval cap.

## Plans fail on sizing and missed days, not on missing reminders

**Students need the schedule made for them, and they cannot size it.** Asked how they decide what to study next, 56 to 59% of students say whatever is due soonest and only 11 to 13% follow a schedule; low performers are the most deadline-driven ([Kornell & Bjork 2007](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf); [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)). University students finish academic tasks 1.2 to 1.8 times later than they predict, are 74 to 84% certain while doing so, and overshoot even their own worst-case forecasts; the correction that removed the bias was tying the prediction explicitly to past experience, and it changed the forecast without changing when anyone finished ([Buehler et al. 1994](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)). Supplying measured past durations improves predictions where memory does not ([Roy et al. 2008](https://jayscholar.etown.edu/facpubharvest/525)). Across roughly 25,000 students, people studied five to eight hours a week less than they planned ([Oreopoulos & Petronijevic 2019](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26059)). The plan should be sized from the student's logged pace, never from stated intentions.

**Task goals work a little; grade goals, nudges and dashboards do not.** Asking about 4,000 undergraduates to set a goal for the number of practice exams raised practice exams completed and course performance by about 0.1 SD, while asking them to set grade goals did nothing, and students met their own task goals only about half the time ([Clark et al. 2017](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)). Against that modest positive stand large nulls: an online planning module plus weekly reminders and text coaching for more than 9,000 students produced "precise null effects on credit accumulation, course grades, and retention" ([Oreopoulos et al. 2022](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/57/1/1)); planning prompts across 269,169 online learners raised engagement for two weeks and completion not at all ([Kizilcec et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)); a review of 38 learning-analytics dashboard studies found no evidence they improve achievement ([Kaliisa et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042)); and thirteen weekly exams with feedback left students as overconfident as before ([Foster et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-016-9158-6)). If-then plans average d = 0.65 in small studies ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)), but they backfire when made for six goals instead of one ([Dalton & Spiller 2012](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html)), and concrete planning reduces effort in people who believe they are behind ([Townsend & Liu 2012](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-665053.html)). Data should drive what is scheduled today; showing it to the student and waiting achieves nothing.

**A missed day does damage when the plan turns it into a violation.** In a five-week field experiment, people asked for seven days a week with two optional "emergency skips" hit their daily goal on 4.0 days a week, against 2.8 for a plain seven-day goal and 3.1 for an easier five-day goal, and the day after a miss they recovered 55% of the time against 37% ([Sharif & Shu](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)). A visibly broken streak lowers subsequent engagement, less so when a repair action is offered ([Silverman & Barasch 2023](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)). Binding weekly study-hour quotas cut completion of a 75-hour study requirement from over 60% to about 40%, as described in a later replication paper ([Hyndman & Bisin](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf)). Fixed time slots underperformed "any time today" in a large gym trial ([Beshears et al. 2021](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3706)). Most of this is exercise and consumer evidence, and no study compares piling up a backlog with redistributing it in a study app, but three independent lines agree on the mechanism. A review backlog that grows with every missed day is the same structure as an unreachable quota.

**One famous result must be dropped.** The claim that imposed, evenly spaced deadlines beat self-set ones rests on Ariely and Wertenbroch (2002). In August and September 2026 Data Colada reported that the data in both of its studies were altered or fabricated ([Data Colada 138](https://datacolada.org/138); [Data Colada 139](https://datacolada.org/139)), and a preregistered replication found deadline type had negligible effect ([Hyndman & Bisin](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf)). What survives is that students want commitment devices and that an opt-in limit on distracting sites raised completion of an online course by 40% where reminders did nothing ([Patterson 2018](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2018-patterson.pdf)).

**Explanation and choice help modestly.** Giving a reason for a task raises perceived value, engagement and performance by d = 0.16 to 0.40, most for dull tasks and when the reason supports the student's own goal instead of commanding ([Steingut et al. 2017](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/2017_SteingutPatallTrimble_MotivSci.pdf)); two to four small choices motivate more than one or than five and above ([Patall et al. 2008](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2008_PatallCooperRobinson_PsychBulletin.pdf)). The streak evidence is company-reported: letting one lesson keep a Duolingo streak raised day-14 retention by 3.3% while fewer learners reached their full daily goal ([Duolingo 2020](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). No study gives a right number of daily tasks or a minimum session length.

## The ledger: retrieval, repetition and spacing are proven; the calendar is custom

## Slap owns every ingredient but schedules almost none of them

Read against that ledger, the current app implements the techniques as features and leaves the scheduling to chance. File and line references below are from the code inventory read on 10 October 2026 and may have shifted since. The plan the student sees is written by a language model from a payload that omits which lecture each concept came from, when anything was last studied, any attempt or review history, the student's available time and whether earlier days were done (`apps/api/src/pipeline/tasks.ts:34-59`); it is cached under a fingerprint that ignores study activity (`studyPlan.ts:110`), its tasks are plain text and not links (`apps/web/src/pages/Today.tsx:217-226`), and its minutes are a model guess between 20 and 120 (`tasks.ts:215`). The rule-based seven-day plan that could carry real parameters is computed and never rendered (`plan.ts:34-97`). Practice questions, which hold the application-level retrieval, are chosen weakest-first with only a 12-hour repeat filter and no spacing or criterion (`practice.ts:95-121, 149`), and a correct answer schedules nothing (`practice.ts:185-193`). Cards run on `fsrs()` defaults with no reference to any exam (`cards.ts:46`), and every unseen card in the class becomes due the moment the student opens Today (`cards.ts:93-98`). Mastery has no time term, so five correct answers in one sitting read as mastered (`mastery.ts:39-44`), which is the illusion the Roediger and Karpicke data describe. The recall check is reachable only from the ended-lecture card and the lecture page, and its "wrong" statements feed nothing (`recall.ts:43, 62`); pretest answers are discarded (`pretest.ts:92-98`). Quizzes are merged into exams with the kind dropped (`syllabus.ts:50-53`), all day arithmetic is UTC (`util.ts:160`), and no table can hold a time budget, a completed day or a missed one. `PLAN.md:246` already says derived pages should read from stored data and not call the model again; the first rule below restores that intent.

The rules are graded A (replicated experiments or meta-analyses including university courses), B (experiments or field data from an adjacent setting, or correlational course data), C (convention or documented product practice) and D (untested inference). Where a rule mixes grades, the principle and the parameter are graded separately.

Two cautions apply to the whole list. Rule 3's evidence comes from definition-heavy psychology courses; for problem-solving courses the measured gain is small, and the app would need generated problem variants with worked solutions before the ladder is worth as much there. And cards and the ladder are parallel retrieval tracks on the same concepts; rule 4's budget and rule 5's order are what stop them doubling the load, so the ladder should not ship without them.

## Conclusion

The evidence reorders the product's own priorities. The scheduling questions that attract engineering effort (which FSRS version, what retention target, how to bend intervals toward an exam) are the ones with no outcome evidence at all, while the plain question of whether a student retrieves each lecture's concepts on four separate days has a 10-point answer. Slap's advantage is that it holds the three inputs the successive relearning researchers had to arrange by hand: the day each concept was taught, a concept-level record of what was said, and the date of the exam that covers it. The plan's job is to convert those into a short, sized, clickable queue that survives a missed day. Prose about strategy, however well written, sits on the side of the ledger where the large trials found nothing.

The same data make Slap an unusually good place to settle what the literature has not. Every quiz and exam date is a scheduled test of the app's own predictions, so the untested rules above (the exam pass, the gap table beyond two sessions, the share for old material, the size of the daily list) can be shipped as experiments with a real outcome attached. That is a stronger position than any published scheduler has reported, and it argues for building the measurement in rule 18 earlier than its tier suggests.
