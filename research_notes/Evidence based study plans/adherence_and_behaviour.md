# Adherence and behaviour: what makes students actually follow a study plan

Research notes compiled 2026-10-10. Audience: software team deciding how a study app presents, sizes and adapts a daily plan for university students over a term.

Verification key used on each finding:
- **[primary text]** = I extracted the numbers from the paper's own full text (PDF) or the publisher/PMC page.
- **[abstract]** = from the paper's abstract or repository record only.
- **[secondary]** = from a press summary, blog or later paper describing the study; treat numbers as unconfirmed.

Population flags: **[uni]** university students, **[MOOC]**, **[school]** schoolchildren, **[non-academic]** exercise, diet, consumer or lab tasks.

## 1. Planning fallacy and time estimation: how badly do students underestimate, and which corrections work?

### Takeaway
University students finish their own academic tasks roughly 1.2x to 1.8x later than they predict, are confident while doing so, and even their "worst case" forecasts are too optimistic. The corrections with evidence are (a) feeding people actual past durations rather than relying on memory, (b) forcing an explicit link between past experience and the current prediction, and (c) unpacking a task into steps. Asking people to merely "remember past experiences" does not work, and no correction in these studies changed when people actually finished.

### Cited Findings
- **Honours thesis study (N = 37 psychology students) [uni][primary text].** Students predicted 33.9 days, took 55.5 days (t(32) = 3.43, p < .002). Only 29.7% finished by their best-guess date. "If everything went as well as it possibly could" estimate: 27.4 days (10.8% finished by then). "If everything went as poorly as it possibly could": 48.6 days, still short of the 55.5 actual, and only 48.7% finished by their own worst-case date. Buehler, R., Griffin, D., & Ross, M. (1994). Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 67*(3), 366-381. — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)
- **Everyday academic and non-academic tasks, same paper Study 2 [uni][primary text].** Academic projects: predicted 5.8 days, actual 10.7 days (F(1,87) = 20.00, p < .001); only 37.1% finished in the predicted time. Non-academic projects: predicted 5.0, actual 9.2 days; 42.5% finished in predicted time. Students reported being 74.1% certain they would meet their academic prediction. Bias was the same whether or not participants knew the study was about prediction accuracy. — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)
- **Why: people plan forward and ignore the past, Study 3 think-aloud [uni][primary text].** 71% of verbalised thoughts were about plans and likely progress, 3% about potential impediments (t(66) = 18.03, p < .001); thoughts about own past experience, others' experience and deadlines each did not exceed a mean proportion of .15. Only 43.6% finished within predicted time, while reporting 83.5% certainty; certainty was not significantly correlated with finishing on time. Predicted and actual times still correlated strongly (r = .81), i.e. students rank tasks well but are biased on level. — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)
- **Correction that worked: explicitly linking past experience to the prediction, Study 4 (n = 41/42/40) [uni][primary text].** Control: predicted 5.5 days, actual 6.8, 29.3% finished on time. "Recall" condition (just remember past experiences): predicted 5.3, actual 6.3, 38.1% on time, not different from control. "Recall-relevant" (recall past experiences and describe a scenario connecting them to this task): predicted 7.0, actual 7.0, 60.0% on time (chi-square = 7.63, p < .01). Important nulls: the manipulation changed predictions but "did not influence when they finished"; absolute prediction error was unchanged (1.8 / 2.0 / 1.9 days), so the bias was removed but not the scatter. — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)
- **Deadlines moved behaviour, predictions did not, same Study 4 [uni][primary text].** Students given a 2-week deadline finished at 9.1 days versus 4.3 days for a 1-week deadline (F(1,117) = 67.26, p < .001), and predicted accordingly (7.9 vs 3.9 days). Absolute prediction error was larger with the longer deadline (2.7 vs 1.1 days). — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)
- **Bias is specific to one's own tasks [abstract].** The paper's abstract: people "underestimate their own but not others' completion times". — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)
- **Feedback on actual past duration improves predictions [non-academic and essay task][abstract].** In three experiments (paper-counting task, essay-writing task, and members of the public given the average duration for others), "supplying feedback increased predictive accuracy". The authors' account is that bias comes from misremembering how long past tasks took, so predictions should be based on measured durations (own or others'), not memory. Roy, M. M., Mitten, S. T., & Christenfeld, N. J. S. (2008). Correcting memory improves accuracy of predicted task duration. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 14*(3), 266-275. — [Roy et al. 2008 record](https://jayscholar.etown.edu/facpubharvest/525)
- **Unpacking a task into sub-steps lengthens estimates and reduces the bias [secondary].** Described in later work as: when a task is unpacked into procedural steps, people give longer completion estimates and the planning fallacy is minimised (Kruger & Evans 2004). A caveat reported in a secondary source: unpacking and similar procedures remove the consistent underestimate rather than making individual predictions accurate (people become about as likely to overestimate). — [Waterloo thesis summarising Kruger & Evans](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/6863); [secondary PDF](https://ourapps.deptcpanel.princeton.edu/uploads/190805145602-A-7320.pdf)
- **Students also over-plan total study hours [uni][abstract].** Across roughly 25,000 students at three campuses (the December 2020 draft says nearly 20,000), students studied "about five to eight hours fewer each week than they plan to", and five years of online and text-message interventions did not close that gap. Oreopoulos, P., & Petronijevic, U. (2019). The remarkable unresponsiveness of college students to nudging and what we can learn from it. NBER Working Paper 26059. — [NBER w26059](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26059)
- **Students stay overconfident about exam scores even with repeated feedback [uni][abstract/secondary].** Across thirteen weekly exams with feedback, students' score predictions remained overconfident and accuracy did not improve; the authors attribute this partly to students not using knowledge of their prior exam performance. Foster, N. L., Was, C. A., Dunlosky, J., & Isaacson, R. M. (2017). Even after thirteen class exams, students are still overconfident. *Metacognition and Learning, 12*, 1-19. — [doi:10.1007/s11409-016-9158-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-016-9158-6)

### Inferences
- **Do not ask the student how long things will take and trust the answer.** Observed ratios of actual to predicted were 1.64 (thesis), 1.84 (week-scale academic tasks), 1.24 and 1.18 (short assignments). A default multiplier of roughly 1.25x to 1.8x on self-estimates is consistent with these data, larger for big, distant, ill-defined tasks. This is my arithmetic on Buehler's means, not a figure the authors propose.
- **Use the student's own logged pace as the estimate source.** Roy et al. (measured durations beat memory) and Buehler Study 4 (bias removed only when past experience is explicitly tied to the forecast) both point to the app computing "you usually take N minutes per card / per page / per problem set" and sizing the day from that, rather than from stated intentions. The app can do the "recall-relevant" step automatically, which students do not do spontaneously (only 12.5% mentioned past experience even when instructed).
- **Removing bias is not the same as getting the work done.** In Study 4 the corrected group predicted accurately but finished no earlier. Honest sizing prevents the plan from being unachievable; it does not by itself increase study. Deadlines did shift completion (9.1 vs 4.3 days).
- **Students' relative ordering is good (r = .60 to .81).** Letting students rank or flag which tasks are bigger is informative; it is the absolute level that needs correcting.
- **Buffers:** even pessimistic estimates undershoot, so a buffer derived from the student's own "worst case" is not enough. Buffer from data (for example the student's 70th-80th percentile past duration) rather than from asked pessimism. This is an inference; I found no study testing percentile buffers in students.
- **Unpacking is cheap in an app:** showing a task as its component steps is likely to raise the time estimate toward reality.

### Gaps
- Buehler et al. measured calendar completion date, not hours on task. I found no equally clean primary source giving the ratio of predicted to actual study minutes per session for university students.
- I could not open Kruger & Evans (2004) or Buehler, Griffin & Peetz (2010); unpacking evidence above is secondary and has no effect size here.
- I found no controlled test of reference-class forecasting as such in student study planning. Roy et al. (others' average duration helps) is the closest.
- Exact statistics for Roy et al. (2008) and Foster et al. (2017) were not retrieved (abstract level only).

## 2. Implementation intentions and specific plans; mental contrasting (WOOP / MCII)

### Takeaway
If-then plans ("when X, I will do Y") have a medium-to-large average effect in small controlled studies (d = .65 in 2006; d = .27 to .66 across outcome types in the 2024/25 update), and are stronger when contingent in format, rehearsed, and when the person already wants the goal. But when planning prompts were scaled to hundreds of thousands of online learners the completion effect fell to roughly zero, plans made for many goals at once backfire, and planning can demotivate students who believe they are already behind.

### Cited Findings
- **Headline meta-analysis [abstract/secondary].** 94 independent tests from 63 reports, more than 8,000 participants: implementation intentions had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = .65 (95% CI reported in one source as 0.6 to 0.7). Most comparisons were against a control that formed only the goal intention. Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38*, 69-119. — [KOPS record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)
- **Updated meta-analysis, 642 tests [abstract].** Effective for cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes (.27 ≤ d ≤ .66). "Effect sizes were larger when plans had a contingent (if-then) format, participants were highly motivated to pursue the goal, and plans were rehearsed." Sheeran, P., Listrom, O., & Gollwitzer, P. M. (2024/2025). The when and how of planning: Meta-analysis of the scope and components of implementation intentions in 642 tests. *European Review of Social Psychology, 36*(1), 162-194. — [KOPS record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905)
- **Academic field example: difficult goals [uni][abstract].** In a correlational study, difficult goals were completed about three times more often when students had also formed implementation intentions; an experiment assigning the same difficult goal reproduced the benefit. Gollwitzer, P. M., & Brandstätter, V. (1997). Implementation intentions and effective goal pursuit. *JPSP, 73*(1), 186-199. Popular summaries quote different percentages for the Christmas-report task (71% vs 32% and 62% vs 22% both circulate); I could not verify either against the paper. — [Gollwitzer & Brandstätter 1997 PDF](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/gollwitzer_brandstatter_1997_-_implementation_intentions_effective_goal_pursuit.pdf)
- **Class attendance [uni][abstract].** Students who wrote when/where/how plans to attend class attended more; the effect was larger for students low or moderate in conscientiousness than for highly conscientious students. Webb, T. L., Christian, J., & Armitage, C. J. (2007). Helping students turn up for class. *Learning and Individual Differences*. — [White Rose record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/10755/)
- **MCII and exam practice [school][abstract].** 66 second-year high-school students preparing for a high-stakes exam were randomised to a 30-minute written mental contrasting with implementation intentions exercise or a placebo writing task. "Students in the intervention condition completed more than 60% more practice questions than did students in the control condition." Duckworth, A. L., Grant, H., Loew, B., Oettingen, G., & Gollwitzer, P. M. (2011). Self-regulation strategies improve self-discipline in adolescents. *Educational Psychology* (2011). — [Duckworth et al. 2011 PDF](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/sites/default/files/duckworth_self-regulation_11.pdf)
- **MCII in middle school [school][abstract].** 77 fifth graders at an urban middle school randomised to MCII or a positive-thinking control; MCII children showed gains in report-card grades, attendance and conduct. Duckworth, A. L., Kirby, T., Gollwitzer, A., & Oettingen, G. (2013). — [PMC4106484](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4106484/)
- **Plans for many goals backfire [non-academic][abstract/secondary].** "The benefits of implemental planning for attaining a single goal do not typically extend to multiple goals." Planning draws attention to how hard it is to execute several goals, which undermines commitment. In one experiment participants pursued either one or six everyday goals daily for a week; implementation intentions helped with one and hurt with six. Framing the set as manageable (for example believing others juggle more) restored the benefit. Dalton, A. N., & Spiller, S. A. (2012). Too much of a good thing: The benefits of implementation intentions depend on the number of goals. *Journal of Consumer Research, 39*(3), 600-614. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120516152448.htm)
- **Planning hurts people who feel behind [non-academic][abstract].** Across five experiments, concrete planning improved subsequent self-control for people who saw themselves in good standing on the goal, but people who saw themselves in poor standing exerted "less self-control after planning than in the absence of planning", because a concrete plan creates emotional distress. Prompting positive self-related thoughts in the domain after planning removed the harm. Townsend, C., & Liu, W. (2012). Is planning good for you? *Journal of Consumer Research, 39*(4), 688-703. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-665053.html)
- **MOOC planning prompt, positive [MOOC][abstract].** In three HarvardX courses, open-ended planning prompts at course start "increased course completion by 29%, and payment for certificates by 40%"; the effect was largest for students enrolled in traditional schools. A surprising detail: students whose plans focused on specific times were less likely to complete. Yeomans, M., & Reich, J. (2017). Planning prompts increase and forecast course completion in massive open online courses. *LAK '17*. — [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/137973)
- **MOOC planning prompt, replication null [MOOC][abstract].** Four large randomised trials found "an overall null effect on the completion rate, ruling out effect sizes beyond the [-7%, 3%] interval"; one course showed a 19% increase. Andor, M. A., Fels, K. M., Renz, J., & Rzepka, S. Do planning prompts increase educational success? Ruhr Economic Papers. — [RWI record](https://www.rwi-essen.de/en/publications/scientific/ruhr-economic-papers/detail/do-planning-prompts-increase-educational-success-evidence-1051)
- **At scale: 269,169 learners, 247 courses, Harvard/MIT/Stanford, 2016-2019 [MOOC][primary text].** Pre-registered completion effects were null: long-term plan-making among committed English-fluent learners +0.19 percentage points (95% CI [-0.69, 1.07], P = .670) in year 1 and -0.23 pp ([-1.61, 1.14]) in year 2; MCII in individualist countries +0.25 pp ([-0.87, 1.37], P = .662). For comparison, the original small studies had plan-making raising completion from 14% to 18% and MCII from 26% to 30%. Self-regulation interventions did raise engagement in weeks 1-2 (for example long plans, week 1, year 2: beta = 0.1057 on a log scale, P < .001), but "these treatment effects were short-lived". The authors conclude that scaling "can reduce their average effectiveness by an order-of-magnitude". A machine-learning policy for targeting who gets which intervention yielded 13.38% completion versus 13.08% for random assignment and 12.81% for none, not significantly better. Kizilcec, R. F., Reich, J., Yeomans, M., et al. (2020). Scaling up behavioral science interventions in online education. *PNAS, 117*(26), 14900-14905. — [PMC7334459](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)

### Inferences
- **A when/where/what plan is worth collecting once, in if-then form, for one anchor behaviour** ("When I finish dinner on weekdays, I open today's session"). The moderators that raise the effect (if-then format, rehearsal, pre-existing motivation) are all things an app can do: store the cue, repeat it back in reminders, and only ask students who have set a goal.
- **Do not ask for a detailed plan per task.** Dalton & Spiller implies that itemised implementation intentions for six things a day make the day look hard and reduce follow-through. One trigger for "the session", then a short list inside it, fits the evidence better than a schedule slot per item.
- **Expect a week-one bump, not a term-long effect, from a one-off onboarding plan.** Kizilcec et al. show engagement gains in weeks 1-2 and no completion effect. Anything meant to last a term has to be re-delivered (rehearsal), not asked once.
- **Be careful showing a concrete catch-up plan to a student who is behind.** Townsend & Liu predicts that the detailed plan itself is what demotivates people in poor standing. Pair any replan with evidence of what the student has done.
- **Specific clock times may be brittle.** Yeomans & Reich found time-specific plans predicted non-completion (correlational). An event-based cue ("after my last lecture") may be more robust than "19:00", though the meta-analysis taxonomy does treat time-and-place cues as a standard, effective cue type.
- **Effects are largest for the least conscientious students** (Webb et al.), which is the group a study app most needs to help.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain domain-level effect sizes for academic outcomes from either meta-analysis; a figure of d = 0.57 for academic achievement appears in a glossary page without a traceable source and should not be cited.
- No university-level randomised trial of WOOP/MCII with grades as the outcome was found; the positive MCII evidence is from schoolchildren and the large-scale MOOC test was null.
- The 2024/25 meta-analysis moderator statistics were not retrieved beyond the abstract.

## 3. Goal-setting: task goals versus grade goals, proximal versus distal, and how big a daily goal should be

### Takeaway
In the best field evidence with university students, asking them to set goals for a concrete task (number of practice exams) raised task completion and course performance by about 0.1 SD, while asking them to set grade goals did nothing detectable. Near-term, bounded goals outperform distant ones, daily goals induce more effort than a single weekly goal, and students set goals that are too ambitious (only about half hit their own task goals, a quarter their grade goals).

### Cited Findings
- **Task-based versus performance-based goals, about 4,000 undergraduates [uni][primary text, NBER 2017 version].** Two separate field experiments in a semester-long on-campus introductory course at a US public university (98% consent). *Task-based:* treated students were asked to set a goal for how many online practice exams they would complete before each midterm and the final. This raised practice exams completed by 0.491 exams (p = .017), "about 0.1 of a standard deviation, or almost six percent", and raised course performance by 0.742 points, "about 0.1 of a standard deviation" (significant at 5% in two specifications and 10% in two). A within-student estimate in the control group put the return to one practice exam at 1.486 points, which predicts 0.730 points from the extra practice, "remarkably close" to the observed effect. *Performance-based:* asking students to set goals for letter grade and exam scores "had positive but small and statistically insignificant effects" (p = .234 and p = .856 for the two goal types). Clark, D., Gill, D., Prowse, V., & Rush, M. Using goals to motivate college students: Theory and evidence from field experiments. NBER Working Paper 23638 (2017); the 2016 IZA version describes the performance-goal result as no discernible impact. — [NBER w23638 PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)
- **Heterogeneity, same study [uni][primary text].** Men: about one extra practice exam (about 0.2 SD, almost 11%), performance up almost 0.2 SD, "almost two points", and almost 10 percentage points more likely to get A- or better. Women: less than 0.2 of an exam, not significant; performance +0.02 SD. Control-group women already completed 17% more practice exams than men; in treatment the gap shrank to 7%. — [NBER w23638 PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)
- **How ambitious self-set goals were, same study [uni][primary text].** Students set average performance goals of 90% and task goals of four out of five practice exams. "Each performance-based goal was reached by about one-quarter of students while each task-based goal was reached by about one-half of students." Treated students were reminded of their goal in reminder emails three days before each exam and when they opened the practice exams. — [NBER w23638 PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)
- **Correlation misleads on grade goals, same study [uni][primary text].** Students who set more ambitious performance goals did perform better (correlation 0.203, p = .000), but the causal effect of being asked to set them was nil; the authors conclude "correlational analysis gives a misleading impression of the effectiveness of performance-based goals". Their proposed reasons: performance is realised in the future so present-biased students discount missing it; students are overconfident about how effort converts to performance; and the effort-to-grade link is noisy, which makes a grade goal risky. — [NBER w23638 PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)
- **Daily versus weekly goals [non-academic online real-effort task][abstract].** Participants randomly assigned to set daily work goals for a week set a higher aggregate goal and "provided more effort compared to the treatment with a weekly goal"; the higher effort was driven by the higher aggregate goal. Theory: a weekly goal lets a present-biased person excuse a slack day by promising to make it up later, whereas daily goals remove that excuse. An added externally enforced minimum daily work requirement lowered average performance because of high dropout (working-paper version). Koch, A. K., & Nafziger, J. (2020). Motivational goal bracketing: An experiment. *Journal of Economic Theory* (doi:10.1016/j.jet.2019.104949). — [CEPR DP13806](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP13806.pdf)
- **Proximal versus distal goals [school][abstract].** Children with marked deficits and disinterest in mathematics did self-directed learning under proximal sub-goals, distal goals or no goals. Proximal sub-goals produced fast progress, substantial mastery, higher self-efficacy and intrinsic interest; "distal goals had no demonstrable effects". Proximal goals also made self-efficacy judgments more accurate. Bandura, A., & Schunk, D. H. (1981). Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation. *JPSP, 41*(3), 586-598. — [Bandura & Schunk 1981 PDF](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf)
- **A high daily goal can block the habit [language-learning app, company-reported A/B test].** Before the change, Duolingo learners with the highest ("intense") daily goal were the least likely to be on a streak, and made up almost 40% of learners who were active two days running but had no streak. Letting one lesson extend the streak, with the daily goal tracked separately, gave +3.3% day-14 retention, +1% daily active learners and +10.5% daily learners on a streak (all relative), +19% for new learners; the trade-off was that fewer learners reached their daily goal. The author's reading: establishing a goal "appeared to be a barrier to forming a daily habit". Yu, A. (2020-11-19). Improving the streak. Duolingo Blog. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)

### Inferences
- **Make the unit of the daily plan a countable task the student controls** (N practice questions, one past paper, 20 cards), not an outcome ("be ready for the midterm") or a grade. Clark et al. is the strongest university evidence in this file and it favours exactly this.
- **Choose the task with a known payoff.** The Clark result worked because practice exams genuinely raise scores (1.486 points each). A task goal on a low-value activity (rereading) would raise completion of the wrong thing.
- **Expect small effects.** 0.1 SD overall, concentrated in men. A study app's goal feature will not by itself transform grades; it is one cheap, positive lever.
- **Students over-set.** Half missed their own task goals and three quarters their grade goals. An app that lets students pick the daily load should default lower than they ask for, or size from logged pace (section 1), because habitual failure to hit a self-set target is the expected outcome otherwise.
- **Separate the "minimum to count the day" from the "target for the day".** The Duolingo test and Koch & Nafziger's minimum-requirement dropout both suggest that a demanding all-or-nothing daily bar costs adherence, while a daily (not weekly) goal frame adds effort. A two-level day (tiny floor, fuller target) is consistent with both; I found no academic RCT testing that exact structure.
- **Keep goals daily or near-term and tie them to the next assessment**, which is how the Clark task goals were framed (per upcoming exam) and what Bandura & Schunk found for proximal sub-goals.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that varies the size of a daily study goal (minutes or items) for university students and measures adherence over a term. The "how big" answer is therefore inferential.
- Clark et al. numbers above are from the 2017 working paper; I did not check them against the 2020 journal version.
- Bandura & Schunk is 1981, with young children and arithmetic; sample size and statistics were not retrieved.
- The Duolingo figures are company-reported, relative, not peer reviewed, and concern language learners in a consumer app.

## 4. Procrastination: prevalence, causes and interventions that work

### Takeaway
Procrastination is the norm among students, and its strongest correlates are task aversiveness, impulsiveness/distractibility, low self-efficacy and distance to the deadline rather than anxiety or perfectionism. The classic "evenly spaced deadlines beat self-imposed deadlines" result should no longer be used: in August-September 2026 Data Colada reported evidence that both studies in Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002) rest on altered data, a preregistered replication failed, and other field experiments find that imposed interim deadlines do not help and can hurt. What survives: students want commitment devices, a software commitment device raised MOOC completion, and concrete task goals raise task completion.

### Cited Findings
- **Prevalence [uni][primary text, preprint].** "Estimates indicate that 80% to 95% of college students engage in procrastination ..., approximately 75% consider themselves procrastinators ..., and almost 50% procrastinate consistently and problematically." Students report it "typically occupies over one third of their daily activities". In the general population it chronically affects "some 15-20% of adults". Steel, P. (2007). The nature of procrastination: A meta-analytic and theoretical review of quintessential self-regulatory failure. *Psychological Bulletin, 133*(1), 65-94. Note these prevalence figures are Steel citing older surveys (Ellis & Knaus 1977; Potts 1987; Solomon & Rothblum 1984 and others), not his own estimates. — [Steel 2007 record](https://prism.ucalgary.ca/handle/1880/47914); [preprint PDF mirror](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/dspace.ucalgary.ca/d0e2e3ffb1b3a53dd76867a247dd2c7a641dc9a6.pdf)
- **Correlates, average r with procrastination (K studies, N) [primary text, preprint tables].** Task aversiveness .40 (K = 8 to 10); impulsiveness .41 (22; 4,005); distractibility .45 (13; 2,232); boredom proneness .39 (3; 408); self-efficacy -.38 (39; 6,994); organisation -.36 (25; 4,757); achievement motivation -.35 (34; 6,171); self-control -.58 (21; 3,840); conscientiousness -.62 (20; 4,012); intention-action gap .29 (6; 533); stated intention -.03 (8; 1,017). Weak: neuroticism .24, fear of failure .18, perfectionism -.03 (24; 3,884), intelligence/aptitude .03. — [Steel 2007 preprint PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/dspace.ucalgary.ca/d0e2e3ffb1b3a53dd76867a247dd2c7a641dc9a6.pdf)
- **Cost to performance [primary text, preprint].** Procrastination correlates with overall performance -.19 (K = 41; N = 7,447), GPA -.16 (19; 4,075), course grade -.25 (10; 2,067), final exam -.17, assignments -.21. — [Steel 2007 preprint PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/dspace.ucalgary.ca/d0e2e3ffb1b3a53dd76867a247dd2c7a641dc9a6.pdf)
- **Temporal Motivation Theory [primary text, preprint].** Utility = (Expectancy x Value) / (sensitivity to delay x Delay); the symbol for sensitivity to delay was lost in my text extraction but the paper names the four variables as "expectancy, value, sensitivity to delay, and delay itself". "As delay becomes large, utility necessarily shrinks"; motivation to write an essay rises only as the deadline nears. Steel lists being organised as helping "with the creation of proximal goals". — [Steel 2007 preprint PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/www/dspace.ucalgary.ca/d0e2e3ffb1b3a53dd76867a247dd2c7a641dc9a6.pdf)
- **Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), original claim [secondary].** Study 2: 60 participants proofread three texts over three weeks; evenly spaced imposed deadlines beat self-imposed deadlines, which beat a single end deadline, on errors detected, lateness and earnings. Study 1: an MIT executive-education class with self-set versus evenly spaced paper deadlines. Ariely, D., & Wertenbroch, K. (2002). Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: Self-control by precommitment. *Psychological Science, 13*(3), 219-224. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/self-imposed-deadlines-dont-stop-procrastination-heres-what-might.html)
- **Evidence of data fraud, Study 2 [primary text of the post].** Data Colada concludes the Study 2 data were "severely tampered with or fabricated to produce the desired results". Evidence: original effect d = 2.5 (136.1 vs 71.1 corrections) with 20 per cell; 18 of 20 participants in the end-deadline condition had a "twin" with identical counts on all three tasks; cross-task performance correlations of +.03 to +.27 in the original versus +.74 to +.90 in the replication. Simonsohn, U., Simmons, J., & Nelson, L. (2026-08-31, updated 2026-09-03). [138] Artificial Deadlines (Part 1). — [Data Colada 138](https://datacolada.org/138)
- **Evidence of data alteration, Study 1 [primary text of the post].** For 13 of 49 self-set-deadline students, spreadsheet grades differ from the recomputed average of components by whole numbers (-20, -10, -6, +4, +10); 12 of 13 changes favour the hypothesis. With recomputed grades the "students who spaced their own deadlines did better" finding reverses (M = 84.91 vs 88.76, t(68) = 2.83, p = .006). "The data were altered after the paper was first submitted." Status as of the post's 2026-09-20 update: Wertenbroch has asked the editor to retract; the journal is considering next steps under COPE guidelines; no retraction confirmed. Simonsohn, Simmons, & Nelson (2026-09-02). [139]. — [Data Colada 139](https://datacolada.org/139)
- **Preregistered replication of Study 2 fails [uni][primary text, preprint].** "Changes in the deadlines have a negligible effect on the three performance metrics and several survey metrics"; "evenly spaced deadlines exogenously imposed on participants do not stand apart for their effectiveness". Demand for commitment did replicate: 93.33% of participants in the self-imposed condition set a binding deadline for tasks 1 and 2, and 89.33% for task 3. An earlier near-identical run with 120 participants gave "virtually identical" results. Hyndman, K., & Bisin, A. Replication of "Procrastination, deadlines, and performance" (preprint dated 2025-09-15; published in *Psychological Science*, 2026). — [preprint PDF](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf); [journal page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261460772)
- **Interim deadlines hurt in a study-hours field experiment [uni][secondary, via the replication paper].** Students had to log 75 hours of monitored studying over five weeks for $95. With binding weekly interim targets (about 12 hours per week for the first four weeks) completion was approximately 40%, versus over 60% with no interim deadlines. Burger, N., Charness, G., & Lynham, J. (2011). Field and online experiments on self-control. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*. — [Hyndman & Bisin preprint describing it](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf); [working paper](https://www.economics.hawaii.edu/research/workingpapers/WP_10-12.pdf)
- **Deadlines did not raise completion; repeated tasks did [uni][abstract].** Over 40% of students displayed present bias. There was "a robust demand for commitment, in the form of self-imposed deadlines", but "deadlines do not increase completion rates", and students did not set deadlines optimally. Present-biased students procrastinated in single-task treatments but "successfully manage to self-control in repeated task treatments". Bisin, A., & Hyndman, K. (2020). Present-bias, procrastination and deadlines in a field experiment. *Games and Economic Behavior* (2020). — [NBER w19874](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19874.pdf)
- **A software commitment device worked; reminders and blockers alone did not [MOOC][abstract/secondary].** Randomised experiment, n = 657, Stanford-hosted MOOC. Students who could pre-set a daily limit on distracting websites (with a morning email and blocking once over the limit) were 40% more likely to complete the course, spent more time and earned higher grades. A reminder triggered by time on distracting sites and a focus tool that blocked sites during coursework were "statistically indistinguishable from the control". Benefits were concentrated in students who said completing the course was very or extremely important. Patterson, R. W. (2018). Can behavioral tools improve online student outcomes? Experimental evidence from a massive open online course. *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 153*. — [Patterson 2018 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2018-patterson.pdf)
- **Treatment-level interventions [abstract].** Meta-analysis of 24 intervention studies (k = 44, N = 1,173): a large reduction in procrastination after intervention, stable at follow-up; cognitive behavioural therapy reduced procrastination more than self-regulation training, other therapies or strengths-based interventions. van Eerde, W., & Klingsieck, K. B. (2018). Overcoming procrastination? A meta-analysis of intervention studies. *Educational Research Review, 25*, 73-85. — [UvA record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/12627c11-4b2f-401a-b9fc-1a7ee4d7cf91)
- **Longer deadline, later finish [uni][primary text].** In Buehler et al. Study 4, a 2-week versus 1-week deadline moved mean completion from 4.3 to 9.1 days. — [Buehler et al. 1994 PDF](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Remove Ariely & Wertenbroch from any design rationale.** The claim "impose evenly spaced deadlines, do not let students choose" now has a failed preregistered replication, a fraud allegation on both studies, and two independent field experiments (Burger et al.; Bisin & Hyndman) pointing the other way.
- **Hard interim deadlines with penalties are risky.** Burger et al.'s weekly quotas cut completion from over 60% to about 40%: a missed week made the whole goal unreachable. This is the academic analogue of a backlog that cannot be cleared (see section 6). Daily goals as reference points (Koch & Nafziger, section 3) are not the same thing as binding deadlines with forfeits.
- **Design to the correlates.** Per Steel's table the levers are: reduce aversiveness of the first step (r = .40), shorten delay to a payoff by making near-term goals and feedback (TMT), protect against distraction (r = .45), and build expectancy of success (r = -.38) with achievable days. Perfectionism (-.03) and fear of failure (.18) are weak targets.
- **Stated intention is uninformative (r = -.03).** A student saying "I'll do two hours tonight" does not distinguish procrastinators from others. Plan from behaviour.
- **Offer commitment as opt-in.** Students clearly demand it (about 90% set binding deadlines when offered) and Patterson's opt-in limit helped the motivated; it did nothing as a passive reminder.
- **Repetition helps present-biased students** (Bisin & Hyndman): a recurring same-shaped daily task is easier to self-control than a one-off large task.

### Gaps
- Steel's prevalence figures trace to surveys from 1977-2000; I did not find a recent, representative prevalence estimate for university students.
- I could not access the published *Psychological Science* replication (403); numbers above are from the authors' preprint, and the exact sample size of the final run was not extracted (the power analysis planned 40 per treatment).
- The Burger et al. completion rates come via a secondary description, not their paper.
- No effect size retrieved for van Eerde & Klingsieck; its studies are therapy-style programmes, not app features.
- "Breaking tasks down" has theoretical support (TMT, proximal goals) and indirect support (unpacking), but I found no randomised test in university students of task decomposition on procrastination itself.

## 5. Self-regulated learning and metacognition: illusions of competence, and what showing students their own data does

### Takeaway
Most university students do not plan their study (11-13% follow a schedule; 56-59% study whatever is due soonest), most were never taught how to study, they use self-testing mainly as a check rather than as a way to learn, and they misjudge which methods work even after experiencing the result. Giving students feedback or dashboards about their own performance has a poor track record on its own: thirteen exams of feedback did not fix overconfidence, and learning analytics dashboards show negligible-to-small effects on achievement.

### Cited Findings
- **How students decide what to study, N = 472 UCLA introductory psychology students [uni][primary text].** "How do you decide what to study next?": whatever's due soonest/overdue 59%; whatever I feel I'm doing the worst in 22%; "I plan my study schedule ahead of time, and I study whatever I've scheduled" 11%; whatever I haven't studied for the longest time 4%; whatever I find interesting 4%. Study the way you do because a teacher taught you: 20% yes, 80% no. Return to material after the course ends: 14% yes, 86% no. Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2007). The promise and perils of self-regulated study. *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 14*(2), 219-224. — [Kornell & Bjork 2007 PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf)
- **Why students quiz themselves, same survey [uni][primary text].** 68% "to figure out how well I have learned the information"; only 18% "I learn more that way than I would through rereading"; 9% do not usually quiz themselves. If convinced they know an answer while studying: 64% "put it aside and focus on other material", 36% make sure to study or test it again later. Rereading: 60% reread marked sections, 16% whole chapters, 23% not usually. — [Kornell & Bjork 2007 PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf)
- **Dropping items too early, lab studies reviewed in the same paper [uni][primary text].** 56% of participants reported using flashcards, and 75% of those reported dropping cards they think they know. Allowing participants to drop items did not help and tended to hurt (63% vs 59% accuracy in one comparison; 61% no-drop vs 59% drop in another, not significant). 58% of dropped items were dropped after a single correct recall. The average judgment of learning for dropped items was only 51%, although 79% said their strategy was to drop items they felt they knew. — [Kornell & Bjork 2007 PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf)
- **Experience does not correct the belief [uni][primary text].** Learning painters' styles: 78% of participants did better with spaced (interleaved) presentation, "but only 22% of the participants thought they had done better in the spaced condition, even though the JOL took place after the test". — [Kornell & Bjork 2007 PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf)
- **Replication at Kent State, N = 324 (78% first-years) [uni][primary text].** Due soonest 56%; plan schedule ahead 13%; doing worst in 24%. Taught how to study by a teacher: 36%. Strategies used regularly: self-test with questions or practice problems 71%; flashcards 62%; reread chapters/notes 66%; underline/highlight 72%; "cram lots of information the night before the test" 66%; make outlines 22%. Self-reported pattern: space out sessions 47%, one session before the test 53%. Study time: evening 69%, late night 20%, afternoon 11%, morning under 1%; believed most effective: evening 50%, afternoon 27%, morning 15%, late night 9%. Hartwig, M. K., & Dunlosky, J. (2012). Study strategies of college students: Are self-testing and scheduling related to achievement? *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 19*, 126-134. — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- **Which habits track GPA, same study [uni][primary text].** Self-testing with questions/practice problems related to GPA (gamma = .28, p = .001); flashcards not (gamma = -.03, p = .76). Regression: test yourself beta = .18 (p = .003); reread beta = .12 (p = .035); make outlines beta = -.12 (p = .045); study with friends beta = -.11 (p = .044); cramming beta = -.11 (p = .064). Spacing versus massing was not significantly related to GPA (gamma = .12, p = .15). Low performers were more likely to study late at night and were especially driven by impending deadlines. Correlational and self-reported. — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- **Repeated outcome feedback does not recalibrate [uni][abstract/secondary].** Across thirteen weekly exams with immediate feedback, students kept over-predicting their next score; they neither raised performance to meet predictions nor lowered predictions to match performance. Foster, Was, Dunlosky, & Isaacson (2017), *Metacognition and Learning, 12*, 1-19. — [doi:10.1007/s11409-016-9158-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-016-9158-6)
- **Proximal goals improve calibration [school][abstract].** In Bandura & Schunk (1981), goal proximity made children's self-efficacy judgments more accurate relative to later performance. — [Bandura & Schunk 1981 PDF](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/59faaf5b01b9500001e95457/5bc552d85141987915dab842_Bandura%20&%20Schunk,%201981.pdf)
- **Dashboards showing students their own data [mostly uni][abstract].** Systematic review of 38 studies: "no evidence to support the conclusion that LADs have lived up to the promise of improving academic achievement"; most studies reported negligible or small effects, with limited evidence from well-powered controlled experiments, and many simply compared dashboard users with non-users, confounding the effect with engagement. Kaliisa, R., Misiejuk, K., López-Pernas, S., Khalil, M., & Saqr, M. (2024). Have learning analytics dashboards lived up to the hype? *LAK '24*. — [arXiv:2312.15042](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042)
- **Advice about self-regulated learning alone does nothing [MOOC][abstract].** N = 653 learners were shown seven study-strategy recommendations distilled from high performers. "Although most learners rated the study tips as very helpful, the intervention did not improve course persistence or achievement." The authors conclude a single prompt at course start is insufficient and call for adaptive support embedded throughout. Kizilcec, R. F., Pérez-Sanagustín, M., & Maldonado, J. J. (2016). Recommending self-regulated learning strategies does not improve performance in a MOOC. *L@S '16*, 101-104. — [doi:10.1145/2876034.2893378](https://doi.org/10.1145/2876034.2893378)

### Inferences
- **The app's largest behavioural contribution may be doing the scheduling the student does not do.** With 56-59% studying whatever is due soonest and 11-13% following a schedule, a default plan that pulls work forward addresses the modal failure, independent of any motivational feature.
- **Do not let "I know this" end practice on an item.** 64% would set aside material they feel they know, and items dropped after one correct recall are a known error. The scheduler, not the student's feeling of knowing, should decide when an item leaves rotation; a manual "skip, I know it" control should be limited or should trigger a quick test.
- **Frame practice testing as learning, not as a check.** Only 18% believe quizzing teaches more than rereading. This is a place where a short rationale ("retrieving it is what makes it stick") is justified (see section 8).
- **Mastery or calibration displays will not change behaviour by themselves.** Foster et al., the dashboard review and Kizilcec 2016 all show information-only interventions failing. The data should drive the plan (what is scheduled today) rather than be presented for the student to act on.
- **If calibration is shown, show prediction against outcome side by side and close to the event**, since students under-use memory of past performance; and keep goals proximal, which improved judgment accuracy in Bandura & Schunk.
- **Expect evening and late-night use** (89% combined) and expect the weakest students to be the latest and most deadline-driven; reminder timing and session length should fit that.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013), "Self-regulated learning: Beliefs, techniques, and illusions", *Annual Review of Psychology, 64*, 417-444 (server refused connection); nothing above is sourced from it. A copy is listed at [the Bjork lab site](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/RBjork_Dunlosky_Kornell_2012.pdf).
- I found no randomised study in which showing students their own calibration curve or mastery estimate changed subsequent study choices over a term. Evidence here is that outcome feedback alone does not.
- Zimmerman's self-regulated learning model was not researched in this pass.
- Survey data are US introductory psychology students, 2007 and 2012; current app-era habits may differ.

## 6. Missed days and plan repair: what-the-hell effect, flexible versus rigid plans, rescheduling

### Takeaway
A lapse does most damage when the plan frames it as a violation that cannot be undone: the perception of having "blown it" drives further lapses, a broken streak reduces subsequent engagement, and quotas that become unreachable collapse completion. Plans with built-in, limited slack outperform both rigid and easy plans (reaching the daily goal on about 4.0 versus 2.8 to 3.1 days a week), flexible timing beat fixed-time routines in a large exercise trial, and a single missed day does little harm to habit formation.

### Cited Findings
- **What-the-hell effect, origin [non-academic, dieting][secondary].** Polivy and Herman's term for counter-regulation: once restrained eaters believe they have exceeded their limit they eat more, not less. In a 2010 study all participants ate the same pizza slice but it was made to look larger for some; dieters who thought they had blown their limit ate more cookies afterwards, while dieters who thought they were within their limit ate the same as non-dieters. The trigger is the perception of failure, not actual intake. Polivy is quoted that the episode "usually lasts for the day". — [Wikipedia: Counterregulatory eating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterregulatory_eating); [PsyBlog summary](https://www.spring.org.uk/?p=13881)
- **Interpretation of the lapse predicts abandonment [non-academic, weight loss][abstract].** In a behaviour-sequence study, abandoning the effort after a lapse was preceded by dichotomous (all-or-nothing) thinking, whereas perseverance was preceded by a more neutral, flexible interpretation of the lapse. — [University of Strathclyde record](https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/en/publications/risk-factors-for-momentary-loss-of-control-and-subsequent-abandon/)
- **Emergency reserves field experiment, over 240 participants, five weeks, pedometer app [non-academic][primary text, working paper].** Each person's daily step goal was set at 120% of their baseline. Weekly goal conditions: Easy (hit the step goal 5 days), Hard (7 days), Reserve-Weekly (7 days with 2 optional "emergency skips" per week, not rolling over), Reserve-Monthly (7 days with 8 skips across four weeks). Days per week the step goal was reached: Easy 3.11, Hard 2.83, Reserve-Weekly 4.00, Reserve-Monthly 3.82 (Reserve-Weekly vs Hard beta = -1.18, p < .001; vs Easy beta = -.90, p = .005). Mean daily steps: Easy 6,662, Hard 6,678, Reserve-Weekly 7,754, Reserve-Monthly 7,981. Abstract: "up to 20% more steps" and reaching the goal "up to 40% more days". Sharif, M. A., & Shu, S. B. Designing more effective goals by using emergency reserves: A field experiment (working paper); see also Sharif & Shu (2017), The benefits of emergency reserves, *Journal of Marketing Research, 54*(3), 495-509. — [working paper PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf); [UCLA Anderson Review summary](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)
- **Persistence the day after a miss, same experiment [non-academic][primary text].** Probability of hitting the goal the day after failing it: Hard .37, Easy .44, Reserve-Weekly .55, Reserve-Monthly .54 when a skip was applied (Reserve-Weekly vs Hard beta = -.17, p = .001). The authors' explanation: in the Hard condition one miss means the weekly goal is already violated; with a reserve it is not. — [working paper PDF](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Designing-More-Effective-Goals-by-Using-Emergency-Reserves-A-Field-Experiment.pdf)
- **Why reserves beat easy goals [non-academic][secondary].** People try not to spend the reserve because using it carries a small cost, so they aim at the harder reference point (7 days) while not experiencing a single miss as failure. After failing a sub-goal, people with reserve-framed goals perceived more progress, which raised commitment and persistence. Sharif & Shu (2019), Nudging persistence after failure through emergency reserves, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*. — [UCLA Anderson Review summary](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/); [2019 paper PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Sharif-Shu_EmergencyReserveFailure_OBHDP2019.pdf)
- **Broken streaks reduce engagement; repair softens it [non-academic, consumer apps][abstract/secondary].** Seven studies: an intact streak shown in a behaviour log increases subsequent engagement relative to a broken streak shown in the same log, regardless of actual past behaviour. The drop is larger when people blame themselves for the break and smaller when they are given a way to "repair" the streak. Barasch's comment in the university's coverage: messages highlighting a recent failure typically do not work, and people tend to drop off once a streak breaks. Silverman, J., & Barasch, A. (2023). On or off track: How (broken) streaks affect consumer decisions. *Journal of Consumer Research* (doi:10.1093/jcr/ucac029). — [UDSpace record](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb); [CU Boulder coverage](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch)
- **Flexible timing beat fixed routine [non-academic, gym field experiment][abstract].** Paying people only for gym visits inside a planned daily two-hour window produced fewer visits than paying for a visit at any time of day, both during the intervention and after incentives ended; the routine group also showed a larger post-intervention drop. Beshears, J., Lee, H. N., Milkman, K. L., Mislavsky, R., & Wisdom, J. (2021). Creating exercise habits using incentives: The trade-off between flexibility and routinization. *Management Science, 67*(7), 4139-4171. — [INFORMS page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3706)
- **Unreachable quotas collapse completion [uni][secondary].** With binding weekly study-hour targets, completion of a 75-hour, five-week study requirement was about 40% versus over 60% without interim deadlines (Burger, Charness & Lynham 2011). — [description in Hyndman & Bisin preprint](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf)
- **One missed day barely matters for habit strength; long gaps do [non-academic][secondary].** In Lally et al. (2010), "missing one opportunity did not significantly impact the habit formation process, but people who were very inconsistent in performing the behaviour did not succeed in making habits". One press account gives the drop after a single miss as .29 on the automaticity score, described as very small. — [UCL news](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/62104-how-to-stick-to-new-years-resolutions)
- **Self-forgiveness after procrastinating [uni][secondary; correlational].** First-year psychology students (reported as N = 134 in one summary and 119 in another) were followed across two midterms. Those who forgave themselves for procrastinating before the first exam reported less negative affect and procrastinated less before the second; the relationship held for those who had procrastinated heavily the first time. Not randomised. Wohl, M. J. A., Pychyl, T. A., & Bennett, S. H. (2010). I forgive myself, now I can study. *Personality and Individual Differences, 48*(7), 803-808. — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/cure-procrastination-forgive-yourself)
- **Detailed planning demotivates those who feel behind [non-academic][abstract].** See Townsend & Liu (2012) in section 2. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-665053.html)
- **Daily versus monthly planning in a study-skills programme [uni][secondary, unverified].** A reader's excerpt of Baumeister & Tierney's *Willpower* describes Kirschenbaum, Humphrey & Malett (1981): students planning month by month improved study habits more than those planning day by day, persisted with planning longer, and among weaker students showed larger grade gains. I could not locate the original paper or its numbers. — [reader notes on *Willpower*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/19243982-willpower/54983241-krishna-pratap/0863a9d5-97ec-45af-9f71-3d2264054ce7)
- **Restart points [uni gym users; Google searches][abstract/secondary].** Gym visits, diet searches and goal commitments rise after temporal landmarks; secondary sources give about +33% at the start of a week and +47% at the start of a semester for university gym attendance (percentages not verified against the paper). Dai, H., Milkman, K. L., & Riis, J. (2014). The fresh start effect. *Management Science*. — [paper PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Never let a missed day make the plan mathematically or visibly failed.** Three independent lines agree: perceived violation triggers further lapses (Polivy), a visibly broken streak lowers engagement (Silverman & Barasch), and interim quotas that cannot be recovered cut completion by about a third (Burger et al.). A backlog that grows each missed day is the same structure.
- **Re-plan forward rather than stacking.** After a miss, recompute the remaining days from the current state (spread or drop the lowest-value items) so today's list is the same size as a normal day. This follows from section 1 (plans sized to real pace), Townsend & Liu (a big concrete catch-up list demotivates those behind) and Dalton & Spiller (long lists reduce commitment). I found no direct A/B test of "redistribute" versus "pile up" in a study app; this is an inference from adjacent evidence.
- **Build the slack in explicitly and make it scarce.** The reserve design (ask for every day, grant about two skips a week, show how many remain) beat both "7 days" and "5 days" on days achieved and on next-day recovery (.55 versus .37). A study-app equivalent is a visible, limited allowance of skip days or rest days that preserves the streak. Note this is exercise evidence over five weeks.
- **Offer a repair action.** Silverman & Barasch found that the opportunity to repair a streak reduces the damage of a break. A short make-up session that restores the day is consistent with that.
- **Do not message about the failure.** Lead the return screen with what is done and what today's (normal-sized) session is; avoid "you missed 3 days". This follows from Barasch's comment, the dichotomous-thinking finding, and (correlationally) Wohl et al.
- **Plan by day, not by clock slot.** Beshears et al. found a fixed two-hour window performed worse than "any time today". This tempers section 2: keep a cue, but do not fail the day because the slot was missed.
- **A weekly or longer horizon should sit above the daily list.** If the Kirschenbaum account is right, students given only rigid daily plans abandoned planning sooner. Showing the week's shape and allowing movement within it is the cautious reading. Treat as weak evidence.
- **Use natural restart points** (Monday, new teaching week, after an exam) to re-onboard lapsed students with a fresh plan.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests the what-the-hell effect or abstinence-violation effect directly on study plans; the mechanism evidence is from dieting and consumer logs.
- No controlled comparison found of backlog accumulation versus automatic rescheduling in spaced-repetition or study-planner software.
- The Kirschenbaum et al. (1981) finding is secondhand; original citation believed to be *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 40*(5), 941-950, not verified.
- Emergency-reserve evidence is one team's work, in exercise and lab tasks, with volunteers, over five weeks; no academic replication found.
- Lally's missed-day statement is from press material; the exact statistic in the paper was not verified.

## 7. Feedback, streaks, reminders and gamification in learning apps

### Takeaway
Reminders and nudges reliably move short-term engagement by small amounts and almost never move grades or completion: the best university-scale evidence (9,000 and about 25,000 students) shows precise nulls on academic outcomes, and one MOOC scheduling nudge was slightly harmful. Streak evidence is mostly company-reported and shows that lowering the bar for keeping a streak raises retention. Gamification meta-analysis shows small positive effects with unstable motivational and behavioural results.

### Cited Findings
- **Scheduling nudge in a MOOC, N = 18,043 [MOOC][abstract].** A self-directed scheduling nudge had "no effects on near-term engagement" and "weakly significant negative effects on longer-term course engagement, persistence, and performance", concentrated among students who registered close to the first day of class and those with .edu email addresses. Baker, R., Evans, B., & Dee, T. (2016). A randomized experiment testing the efficacy of a scheduling nudge in a massive open online course (MOOC). *AERA Open, 2*(4). — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/003c9cb555344521b6aefb5c20fffa47)
- **Online planning module, reminders and text coaching, more than 9,000 students at three colleges [uni][abstract].** Students were randomised to build a weekly schedule in an online planning module, or to receive weekly study reminders or coach consultation by text. Treated students did somewhat more studying at two of the sites, but the authors "estimate precise null effects on credit accumulation, course grades, and retention at each site" for the full sample and multiple subgroups. Oreopoulos, P., Patterson, R. W., Petronijevic, U., & Pope, N. G. (2022). Low-touch attempts to improve time management among traditional and online college students. *Journal of Human Resources, 57*(1). — [JHR](https://jhr.uwpress.org/content/57/1/1)
- **Five years of nudges, nearly 25,000 students [uni][abstract].** Online and text-message interventions through several channels: coaching-based ones produced some gains in mental health and study time, but "none of the interventions we evaluate significantly influences academic outcomes", including for at-risk students. Students who realised more effort was needed tended to lower their grade expectations rather than study more. The authors judge that "more comprehensive but expensive programs appear more promising". Oreopoulos & Petronijevic (2019), NBER w26059. — [NBER w26059](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26059)
- **Planning prompts at scale [MOOC][primary text].** Null on completion, short-lived early engagement gains; see Kizilcec et al. (2020) in section 2. — [PMC7334459](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)
- **Reminder-style tools [MOOC][abstract/secondary].** In Patterson (2018), a reminder triggered by time on distracting sites and a focus blocker were indistinguishable from control; only the pre-commitment limit raised completion (by 40%). — [Patterson 2018 PDF](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/conscientiousness/2018-patterson.pdf)
- **Optimising reminder content has small effects [language app][abstract].** A bandit algorithm choosing among push-notification templates for millions of daily practice reminders produced "a 0.5% increase in total daily active users (DAUs) and a 2% increase in new user retention" over an already heavily A/B-tested baseline; the design had to account for novelty effects (a template loses effect with repetition and recovers after rest). Yancey, K. P., & Settles, B. (2020). A sleeping, recovering bandit algorithm for optimizing recurring notifications. *KDD '20*. — [Duolingo Research PDF](https://research.duolingo.com/papers/yancey.kdd20.pdf)
- **Streak design, company-reported A/B test [language app].** Decoupling the streak from the daily goal so that one lesson extends the streak: day-14 retention +3.3%, daily active learners +1%, daily learners on a streak +10.5% (new learners +19%), all relative; fewer learners then reached their daily goal. About a year later just over half of daily learners had a streak of 7 or more days, up from about a third. Learners who reached a 7-day streak were 2.4 times more likely to return the next day than those without one (correlational, so subject to selection). — [Duolingo blog, 2020-11-19](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- **Streak visibility, secondhand [language app][secondary].** Showing the streak count permanently at the top of the app: +3% daily active users and +1% day-14 retention; emphasising the streak after each lesson: +1% DAU and +3% day-14 retention. — [Econsultancy](https://econsultancy.com/six-a-b-tests-used-by-duolingo-to-tap-into-habit-forming-behaviour/)
- **Streaks as goals in themselves; breaks demotivate [consumer apps][abstract].** See Silverman & Barasch (2023) in section 6. — [UDSpace record](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb)
- **Gamification meta-analysis [mixed education levels][abstract].** Small significant effects on cognitive learning outcomes (g = .49, 95% CI [0.30, 0.69], k = 19, N = 1,686), motivational outcomes (g = .36, [0.18, 0.54], k = 16, N = 2,246) and behavioural outcomes (g = .25, [0.04, 0.46], k = 9, N = 951). The cognitive effect was stable in a subsample of methodologically rigorous studies; the motivational and behavioural effects were less stable. Sailer, M., & Homner, L. (2020). The gamification of learning: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review, 32*, 77-112. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)
- **Dashboards [mostly uni][abstract].** Negligible-to-small effects on achievement; see Kaliisa et al. (2024) in section 5. — [arXiv:2312.15042](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15042)

### Inferences
- **Treat reminders as a way to trigger a session that is already well designed, not as the intervention.** The repeated pattern is a small engagement lift and no outcome change. Large, well-run trials with real university students found nothing on grades from planning modules plus reminders.
- **Engagement metrics will mislead.** Several studies move activity (study time, early engagement, streak counts) without moving completion or grades. The team should track a learning outcome or at least completion of high-value tasks, not only daily actives or streak length.
- **Nudges can backfire for some users.** Baker et al.'s negative effect was concentrated in specific subgroups; Kizilcec et al. could not predict who benefits. Ship nudges behind experiments and watch subgroups.
- **Rotate reminder wording.** The Duolingo bandit paper exists because repeated templates lose effect. Expected gain is small (0.5% to 2%).
- **If streaks are used, make them cheap to keep and repairable.** The only quantified streak evidence says lowering the requirement to one unit raised retention, at the cost of less daily volume. That trade-off (habit versus daily amount) is a product decision; the evidence does not say which yields more learning.
- **Gamification's benefit is modest and its motivational effect is fragile**; no basis here for expecting points or badges to sustain a term of study.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, quantified evaluation of streak freezes, streak repair or streak wagers was found; Duolingo material is self-reported and relative.
- No randomised evidence found that a streak feature improves learning outcomes (as opposed to retention).
- I did not find a published study of reminder timing or frequency in a university study app with academic outcomes.
- Baker et al. (2016) and Oreopoulos et al. (2022) are cited from abstracts; effect magnitudes and confidence intervals were not extracted.

## 8. Explaining the plan: does giving a rationale ("why this today") improve uptake?

### Takeaway
Giving a reason has small-to-moderate positive effects on perceived value, engagement and performance (d = .16 to .40 across 23 experiments), works best for uninteresting tasks and when the reason is autonomy-supportive or prosocial rather than controlling, but does not raise autonomous motivation and slightly lowers perceived competence. A relevance message that helped in early studies had effects an order of magnitude smaller at scale and was negative in some course contexts.

### Cited Findings
- **Meta-analysis of rationale provision [mixed samples][primary text, abstract page].** 23 experimental studies. "Rationale provision enhanced subjective task value, engagement, performance, and perceived autonomy to a small to moderate extent (d = .16 to d = .40)" under fixed- and random-effects models. It "diminished perceived competence" (d = .19 in magnitude) and "did not impact autonomous motivation, controlled motivation, or perceived relatedness". Moderators: rationales were most effective "(a) when they were prosocial or autonomous compared to controlling (for 3 outcomes), (b) for samples with a higher proportion of females (for 3 outcomes), and (c) for uninteresting tasks (for 3 outcomes)". Steingut, R. R., Patall, E. A., & Trimble, S. S. (2017). The effect of rationale provision on motivation and performance outcomes: A meta-analysis. *Motivation Science, 3*(1), 19-50. — [Steingut et al. 2017 PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/2017_SteingutPatallTrimble_MotivSci.pdf)
- **Choice as autonomy support [children and adults][primary text].** Meta-analysis of 41 studies: providing choice enhanced intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance and perceived competence. The effect on intrinsic motivation was strongest when participants made two to four choices (k = 12, d = 0.61, 95% CI [0.48, 0.75]) compared with a single choice (k = 21, d = 0.21, [0.14, 0.28]) or five or more choices (k = 12, d = 0.32, [0.22, 0.43]) under fixed effects; under random effects two-to-four (d = 0.63) still beat one (d = 0.23) but did not differ significantly from five or more. For options per choice, results favoured three to five options (near-significant; fixed-effects Q(2) = 5.62, p < .06). Effects were stronger for instructionally irrelevant choices and for children than adults, and weaker when rewards followed. Patall, E. A., Cooper, H., & Robinson, J. C. (2008). The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes. *Psychological Bulletin, 134*(2), 270-300. — [Patall et al. 2008 PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2008_PatallCooperRobinson_PsychBulletin.pdf)
- **Value-relevance message at scale [MOOC][primary text].** Asking learners to connect the course to their own values had no overall effect on completion for the targeted group (learners in less-developed countries: +0.14 pp, 95% CI [-0.75, 1.03] in year 1; -0.04 pp in year 2). In courses with a pre-existing global achievement gap it helped (+2.79 pp, [1.30, 4.27], P < .001; +2.74 pp in year 2); in courses without that gap the post hoc estimate was negative (-1.62 pp, P = .004; -1.71 pp, P = .031). "The effect of the intervention is significant but an order-of-magnitude smaller than in our prior study." Whether a course had the gap could not be predicted in advance (54.3% accuracy versus 50% chance). Kizilcec et al. (2020), *PNAS*. — [PMC7334459](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334459)
- **Signalling that a task is worthwhile [uni][primary text].** In Clark et al.'s task-goal experiment the authors took care to make the value of practice exams equally salient to treatment and control, because telling students a task is effective is itself a possible channel. — [NBER w23638 PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)
- **Tips rated helpful, no behavioural effect [MOOC][abstract].** Study-strategy recommendations were rated very helpful by most learners and changed neither persistence nor achievement (Kizilcec et al. 2016, N = 653). — [doi:10.1145/2876034.2893378](https://doi.org/10.1145/2876034.2893378)

### Inferences
- **A one-line "why this today" is justified, with modest expectations** (d about .2 to .4 on value and engagement, nothing on deeper motivation). It should matter most for the dull items, which is where a study plan needs it (for example a retrieval drill the student would rather skip).
- **Word the reason around the student's own goal, not around obligation.** "This is here because it's the topic you scored lowest on and the exam is in 9 days" is autonomy-supportive; "You must complete this to stay on track" is the controlling framing that performed worst.
- **Watch the competence side-effect.** Rationales slightly reduced perceived competence. A reason phrased as a deficit ("you are weak at X") could plausibly amplify that; pairing it with progress evidence is prudent. This is my reading, not a tested moderator.
- **Give a little choice, not a lot.** The Patall result (two to four choices best; three to five options each) suggests letting the student make a few small decisions about the day (order, swap one item, pick between two topics) rather than either a fixed list or an open menu.
- **Do not expect explanation to substitute for structure.** Kizilcec 2016 and the scaled value-relevance test show that telling learners why and how, once, is liked and ineffective.

### Gaps
- I found no experiment that tests explaining an algorithm-generated study plan (or a spaced-repetition schedule) to university students and measures adherence. The rationale literature is mostly teacher- or experimenter-delivered reasons for a single task.
- The per-outcome effect sizes within the d = .16 to .40 range, and how many of the 23 studies involved university students, were not extracted.
- No evidence retrieved on whether repeated daily rationales habituate.

## 9. Overload: optimal number of daily tasks or choices, and short minimum-viable sessions for keeping a habit

### Takeaway
There is no validated "right number" of daily study tasks. What the evidence supports is directional: detailed plans for many goals at once backfire where plans for one goal help, choice is most motivating at two to four decisions with three to five options, choice overload appears mainly when the task is hard and preferences are unclear, and habit strength builds over about two months of consistent repetition with large individual variation and tolerance for the odd miss. The case for very short "minimum" sessions rests on one company A/B test plus habit theory, not on academic trials.

### Cited Findings
- **Many goals plus detailed plans is worse than few [non-academic][abstract/secondary].** One versus six daily goals for a week: implementation intentions helped with one and undermined success with six, because planning made the difficulty of executing all of them salient. Dalton & Spiller (2012). — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-664500.html); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/05/120516152448.htm)
- **Choice overload is not a general law [consumer choice][abstract].** Meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036) found "a mean effect size of virtually zero" with considerable variance between studies; no sufficient conditions for overload could be identified. Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? *Journal of Consumer Research, 37*(3), 409-425. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v37y2010i3p409-425.html)
- **When overload does occur [consumer choice][abstract].** A later meta-analysis identified four moderators: "higher levels of decision task difficulty, greater choice set complexity, higher preference uncertainty, and a more prominent, effort-minimizing goal facilitate choice overload"; with these included the overall effect of assortment size is significant. Chernev, A., Böckenholt, U., & Goodman, J. (2015). When product assortment leads to choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis (title as listed on the Kellogg record). *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 333-358. — [Kellogg record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual)
- **Optimal amount of choice for motivation [children and adults][primary text].** Two to four choices (d = 0.61) beat one (d = 0.21) and five or more (d = 0.32) under fixed effects; three to five options per choice were favoured. Patall, Cooper, & Robinson (2008). — [Patall et al. 2008 PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/2008_PatallCooperRobinson_PsychBulletin.pdf)
- **How long habits take [non-academic; mostly students as participants][abstract/secondary].** 96 volunteers chose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour to do daily in the same context for 12 weeks; 82 provided enough data; an asymptotic curve fitted 62 individuals, 39 of them well. Among those, the modelled time to reach the automaticity plateau had a median of 66 days and ranged from 18 to 254 days (some values are extrapolations beyond the 84 days observed). More consistent performance was associated with better model fit. Lally, P., van Jaarsveld, C. H. M., Potts, H. W. W., & Wardle, J. (2010). How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world. *European Journal of Social Psychology, 40*(6), 998-1009. — [repository record](https://repositorio.ispa.pt/entities/publication/fae72a1c-b2b6-4991-9951-5d20b607f688/full); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit)
- **Missing one day [non-academic][secondary].** "Missing one opportunity did not significantly impact the habit formation process", but very inconsistent performers did not form habits. — [UCL news](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)
- **Lowering the daily bar raised retention [language app, company-reported].** Requiring one lesson rather than the full daily goal to keep the streak: +3.3% day-14 retention, +10.5% learners on a streak (relative); learners on the most demanding daily goal had been least likely to hold a streak. The author's stated rationale: lowering barriers to a consistent daily habit matters more early on than how much is done each day. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)
- **An enforced daily minimum caused dropout [online real-effort task][abstract].** Adding an externally enforced minimum daily work requirement to daily goals lowered average performance because of high dropout (working-paper version of Koch & Nafziger 2020). — [CEPR DP13806](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP13806.pdf)
- **Students' own targets overshoot [uni][primary text].** Self-set task goals averaged four of five practice exams and were met about half the time (Clark et al.). — [NBER w23638 PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23638.pdf)
- **Repetition aids self-control [uni][abstract].** Present-biased students managed self-control in repeated-task treatments where they procrastinated on single tasks (Bisin & Hyndman 2020). — [NBER w19874](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19874.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Show a short list.** No study gives a number, but the convergent direction (one goal fine, six harmful when each is planned in detail; two to four choices optimal; overload when tasks are hard and preferences unclear, which describes studying) supports presenting on the order of two to four items for the day, with anything further collapsed or deferred. The specific count is a design judgment to A/B test, not an evidence-derived constant.
- **Size the day in the student's measured time, then cap the item count.** Section 1 handles how much; this section says keep the visible number of separate decisions small.
- **Define a floor that is easy and not externally punished.** The Duolingo result favours a tiny qualifying unit; Koch & Nafziger's dropout under an enforced minimum warns against making the floor a hard requirement with consequences. A floor that keeps the day "alive" and a target above it fits both.
- **Plan onboarding for months, not weeks.** A median of 66 days with a range of 18 to 254 means a 12-week term is roughly one habit-formation period for a typical student and not enough for many. Features that depend on the habit being established should not assume it before week 8-10.
- **Same context, same cue, daily.** Lally's design (daily, same context) and Bisin & Hyndman's repetition effect both favour a recurring, same-shaped session over variable large blocks.
- **Trade-off to decide deliberately:** the lower bar raised retention and reduced daily volume. For exam-bound students, volume matters; the floor should protect continuity on bad days without becoming the default amount.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on the optimal number of tasks in a daily study plan, or on minimum session length (for example 5 versus 15 versus 30 minutes) and term-long adherence in university students.
- "Tiny habits"-style claims that very small sessions build study habits were not found tested in academic RCTs; the supporting data here are one consumer-app A/B test.
- Lally et al. did not study studying; behaviours were simple health actions, automaticity was self-reported, and I could not verify from the paper itself whether more complex behaviours (exercise) took longer than simple ones (drinking water), which is commonly reported.
- Choice-overload meta-analyses concern product assortments, not task lists; mapping to "number of tasks shown" is by analogy.
