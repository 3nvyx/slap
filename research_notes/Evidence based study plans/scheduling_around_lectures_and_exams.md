# Scheduling study time around lectures and exams: the calendar-level structure of a university study plan

Reading guide. Each finding is tagged:
- **[E]** = empirical evidence (experiment, meta-analysis, or measured field data). Design noted where it limits the claim (lab vs classroom, correlational vs randomized).
- **[C]** = convention / practitioner guidance (learning centres, regulations, rules of thumb). Not tested as stated.
- **(2nd)** = I could only reach the claim through a secondary source or a search-engine abstract, not the paper's full text. Verify before quoting in a user-facing product.

Sources read in full text for this note: Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012; Cepeda et al. 2008; St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024; Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022 (partial); Dunlosky et al. successive-relearning chapter; Osterhage 2021; Walck-Shannon et al. 2021; Smits et al. 2025; Bailey et al. 2021; Cornell LSC five-day plan. Everything else is abstract-level or secondary and is marked.

## 1. Back-scheduling from an exam: how early to start, how to distribute sessions, what the final days contain; cramming vs distributed study

### Takeaway
The best quantitative anchor is Cepeda et al. (2008): the optimal gap between two study sessions grows with the time until the test but shrinks as a fraction of it (about 3 days for a test 1 week away, about 8 days for 5 weeks, about 12 days for 10 weeks), and a gap that is too short costs far more than one that is too long. Classroom trials of "successive relearning" (first session when the lecture introduces the material, then about three more retrieval sessions a few days apart) raise course exam scores by roughly 10 percentage points and multiply retention a few weeks after the exam by about three. "No new material in the last days", tapering, and the specific five-day layouts are convention, not tested findings.

### Cited Findings

**Optimal spacing as a function of time-to-test**
- [E] Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), *Psychological Science* 19(11), 1095-1102. 1,354 web participants learned 32 trivia facts to a criterion of one correct recall each, restudied once after a gap of 0-105 days, and were tested 7, 35, 70 or 350 days after the second session (26 gap x retention-interval cells). — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- [E] Interpolated optimal gaps for recall were "approximately 3, 8, 12, and 27 days (corresponding to 43%, 23%, 17%, and 8% of the RIs)" for retention intervals of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days. For recognition (multiple choice): 1.6, 7, 10 and 25 days (24%, 19%, 14%, 7%). Best gaps among those actually tested: 1, 11, 21, 21 days (recall). — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [E] Size of the effect with study time held constant: the optimal gap vs a zero-day gap "provided a 64% increase in final recall, d = 1.1, and a 26% increase in final recognition, d = 1.5". By retention interval (7, 35, 70, 350 days) the recall improvement was 10%, 59%, 111% and 77%; recognition 1%, 10%, 31%, 60%. — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [E] Asymmetric cost: "although there are costs to using a gap that is longer than the optimal value, these costs are much smaller than the costs of using too short a gap ... as the gap increases, accuracy increases steeply and then declines much more gradually". — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [E] The authors' own educational reading: "if you want to know the optimal distribution of your study time, you need to decide how long you wish to remember something", and confining "study of a single topic ... within a given week of a course" is "likely to produce misleadingly high levels of immediate mastery that will not survive the passage of substantial periods of time". — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [E] Uniform vs expanding intervals: Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021), *Educational Psychology Review* 33, 959-987, meta-analysis of 29 studies: spaced retrieval practice beat massed retrieval practice (g = 0.74); "no clear evidence that expanded spacing works better than uniformly spaced retrieval practice" (a secondary summary reports g = 0.034 for expanding vs uniform; not verified against the paper). (2nd) — [Springer Nature research round-up](https://communities.springernature.com/amp/posts/september-2021-research-round-up)

**Successive relearning in real courses (spacing + retrieval to criterion)**
- [E] Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli (2013), *Educational Psychology Review* 25(4), 523-548, as described by its own authors: students started a virtual flashcard stack (6-8 concepts each) "when the instructor introduced them in class" and relearned it "on three or more sessions that were separated by at least one day"; the same students studied other concepts "business as usual". Result: "Successive relearning boosted exam performance over 10% as compared to the baseline control." The in-class exam questions were application questions, not definition recall. — [Dunlosky, Greve, Badali, Wissman & Rawson, successive relearning chapter (UNH-hosted PDF)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] Retention after the exam, same study: on a definition-recall test "administered 24 days after the exam", students "recalled over 60% of the concepts correctly when they used successive relearning ... whereas they recalled less than 20% when they learned the concepts on their own". The authors read this as showing "that students' reliance on cramming to study course materials results in rapid forgetting". — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] Replication in a harder course: Janes et al. (2020), biopsychology, "about a 10% boost in their exam performance"; Carpenter et al. describe this as grades "enhanced by more than a letter grade after engaging in successive relearning of course information every few days, compared with using their own methods". — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf); [Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022 PDF](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/s44159-022-00089-1.pdf)
- [E] Time-matched comparison: Higham et al. (2022): after each lecture, students practised key concepts "on three separate days" either by successive relearning or by restudy with time equated; criterion tests "about 42 and 68 days after the final practice session" showed significantly better recall for successive relearning at 42 days and a smaller but significant benefit at 68 days. — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] Problem-solving material shows a smaller effect: three sessions on separate days vs one session to the same criterion gave a small advantage one week later ("Cohen's d = .28; average performance was also around 50%"). — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] Dose within the first session: recalling each item correctly three times in session 1 (vs once), followed by three relearning sessions at one correct recall each, gave items "recalled on the first try about 15% better 2 days later in the first relearning session (an effect size of Cohen's d = 0.63)", with the advantage persisting in "relearning sessions 8 and 10 days later". — [Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022, *Nature Reviews Psychology* 1, 496-511 (PDF)](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/s44159-022-00089-1.pdf)
- [E/C] Time cost, from the authors (cites Rawson & Dunlosky 2011): "It may take students 30 to 40 minutes to initially learn to correctly recall eight to ten lengthy definitions, but during the first relearning session, they may need only about 15 minutes, and the next session will likely take fewer than 5 minutes". They advise stacks of "relatively few (8 - 10)" concepts. — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [C, expert recommendation] Scheduling advice from the same authors: put a start date on the calendar "when the concepts are introduced in a lecture" and revisit "for three extra sessions separated by 2 days"; "if each exam is separated by four weeks and covers a great deal of material, that probably leaves just enough time to relearn materials on two (perhaps three) extra sessions"; with a cumulative final, "return to the material previously introduced to engage in one or two relearning sessions near the end of the semester". — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] Spacing schedules that have worked in undergraduate classroom studies (Table 1 of Carpenter et al. 2022): statistics, "three practice sessions, spaced apart by 2 or 5 days"; physics, "three problems per topic spaced apart by 2 days or more"; engineering, "three homework sets spaced apart across 3 weeks"; pre-calculus, "three quizzes spaced apart by 1-2 weeks"; meteorology, "two sessions spaced apart by 8 days". — [Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022 PDF](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/s44159-022-00089-1.pdf)

**Cramming vs spacing: exam score vs long-term retention**
- [E] Kornell (2009), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 23, 1297-1317: three web experiments with GRE-type word pairs on flashcards. "Spacing was more effective than massing for 90% of the participants, yet after the first study session, 72% of the participants believed that massing had been more effective than spacing." Spacing was also more effective than cramming, defined as massing study on the last day before the test. (Abstract-level; per-condition percentages not retrieved.) (2nd) — [Kornell 2009 abstract record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152)
- [E, correlational survey] Hartwig & Dunlosky (2012), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 19, 126-134, N = 324 Kent State undergraduates: 53% "most often do my studying in one session before the test" vs 47% "space out my study sessions over multiple days/weeks"; 66% regularly "'cram' lots of information the night before the test". — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- [E, correlational] Same survey: self-reported spacing and cramming were only weakly and non-significantly related to GPA (spacing gamma = .12, p = .15; cramming gamma = -.16, p = .08; in regression cramming beta = -.11, p = .064). Self-testing was related (gamma = .28, p = .001; beta = .18, p = .003). The authors note the benefit of some strategies shows up mainly at long retention intervals, so "their contributions to exam performance may be limited by many students' propensity to cram the night before tests". — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- [E, correlational] Same survey, how students decide what to study next: 56% "whatever's due soonest/overdue"; 24% "whatever I feel I'm doing the worst in"; 13% "I plan my study schedule ahead of time, and I study whatever I've scheduled"; 2% "whatever I haven't studied for the longest time". Deadline-driven choice was "especially true for low performers"; "high performers are more likely" to schedule ahead. — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- [E, correlational] Walck-Shannon, Rowell & Frey (2021), *CBE-Life Sciences Education* 20(1), ar6; introductory biology, 502 consenting students: students started studying a mean 5.84 days before exam 2 (SD 4.38). Start day did not predict the exam (b = 0.011, SE 0.168, n.s.), a delayed post-test (b = 0.252, n.s.) or the cumulative final (b = -0.184, n.s.), controlling for preparation, absences and total study time. Total hours predicted weakly (0.13 and 0.20 exam points per hour). — [Walck-Shannon et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8108503/)
- [E] Measured behaviour: Taraban, Maki & Rynearson (1999): in both lecture and web-based courses "both beginning and experienced college students reported studying almost exclusively just before exams", and computer logs confirmed the self-reports; weekly quiz deadlines produced a weekly activity pattern. (2nd) — [Taraban, Maki & Rynearson record](https://scholars.ttu.edu/en/publications/measuring-study-time-distributions-implications-for-designing-com-5)
- [E] Review statement: "few students engage in spacing out their studying over time but, instead, tend to 'cram' their studying within a few days of an examination". — [Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022 PDF](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/s44159-022-00089-1.pdf)

**Concrete back-schedules (practitioner convention)**
- [C] Cornell Learning Strategies Center "Five-Day Study Plan". Split material into chunks A-D (A oldest). Day 1: prepare A 2 h. Day 2: prepare B 2 h + review A 30 min. Day 3: prepare C 1.5 h + review B 30 min + review A 15 min. Day 4: prepare D 1 h + review C 30 min, B 15 min, A 15 min. Day 5: review D 25 min, C 15 min, B 10 min, A 10 min + "self-test on A, B, C, D: 1 hr". Total 10 h 45 min (6.5 h prepare, 4 h 15 min review); "about 2 hours studying on each of the five days". "Prepare" = make study guides, flash cards, concept maps, self-written questions; "review" = blank-page testing, quizzing, practice exams. Stated rationale is the general claim that "people learn faster and perform better if they work in brief blocks of time spread out over longer periods"; no study is cited. — [Cornell LSC five-day study plan](https://lsc.cornell.edu/how-to-study/studying-for-and-taking-exams/the-five-day-study-plan/)
- [C] The Cornell page also says: "During the five days you are studying for your exam ... don't neglect your other courses!" Adaptations state that "the last day of study should be the day before the exam" and estimate "eight to ten hours of total studying may be needed for an A or B". (2nd, from search summary of the adapted handouts) — [Vanderbilt five-day plan handout](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-sub/wp-content/uploads/sites/287/2024/05/05132235/5daystudyplan.doc)

### Inferences
- Applying the Cepeda ridgeline to a course: if the exam is T days after the last planned review, the gap before that review should be roughly 0.4T at T = 7, 0.2T at T = 35 and 0.15T at T = 70. In calendar terms: exam 1 week away, re-study after about 2-3 days; exam 5 weeks away, after about 1-1.5 weeks; exam 10 weeks away, after about 2-3 weeks. Because long gaps cost little and short gaps cost a lot, a scheduler that is unsure should round gaps up, and should never place the second exposure on the same day and call it a review.
- Cepeda et al. tested exactly two sessions on trivia facts. A course gives more sessions; the classroom successive-relearning trials (first session at lecture, then 3 sessions about 2 days apart, plus 1-2 boosters before a cumulative final) are the best-tested multi-session template. A reasonable default rule: lecture day (initial learning to 3 correct recalls) then +2, +4 to +6, +8 to +10 days, then one booster about 1-2 weeks before the exam and one in the final 2-3 days.
- The meta-analytic absence of an expanding-interval advantage means the scheduler does not need an exponential interval formula; evenly spaced sessions sized to the days available are defensible.
- The two correlational course studies (Hartwig & Dunlosky; Walck-Shannon et al.) show that "starting earlier" on its own does not predict exam scores. What predicts is what happens in the sessions (self-testing, problem sets, explaining). So "start N days before" should be implemented as "N days of retrieval-based sessions", not as earlier passive review.
- Cramming is not shown to hurt the immediate exam much in survey data; its clear cost is retention after the exam (under 20% vs over 60% at 24 days in Rawson et al.). For courses with cumulative finals or prerequisite chains, that retention difference becomes an exam-score difference later in the term.
- The Cornell plan is a 5-day, about 2 h/day, 60:40 prepare:review template whose review share rises to 100% on the last day. It is consistent with the evidence in direction (spaced, retrieval-heavy, self-test last) but its specific minutes are untested.

### Gaps
- No study found that experimentally varies the start date of exam preparation (e.g. 14 vs 7 vs 3 days) with total time held constant in a real course.
- No direct evidence for "no new material in the final 24-48 hours" or for tapering study volume before an exam. Both are practitioner convention. The nearest evidence is on sleep (section 6) and on practice tests (section 2).
- Kornell (2009) per-experiment percentages and the exact cramming-condition design could not be retrieved (publisher and author PDFs returned 403). Figures circulating on blogs (e.g. 65% vs 38%; 74% vs 32%) conflict with each other and are not verified.
- Rawson et al. (2013) was read only through the authors' later chapter; exact means, Ns and the spaced-restudy comparison were not retrieved.

## 2. Practice exams / mock tests: how many, how far ahead, under what conditions, and effects on performance and calibration

### Takeaway
Practice testing has a medium positive effect on classroom achievement (g about 0.5 across 222 studies), larger when the practice format matches the exam and when corrective feedback is given. Field data link taking a full, timed practice exam to about 4-7 percentage points higher exam scores and better calibration early in the term, but the lowest performers stay overconfident even after practice-test feedback. The number of mock exams and how many days ahead to sit them have not been tested; "one full timed mock a few days before" is convention.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021), *Psychological Bulletin* 147(4), 399-435: 222 independent classroom studies, 48,478 students; quizzing raises academic achievement "to a medium extent (g = 0.499)". Significant moderators listed in the abstract include control-condition strategy, "test format consistency", material matching, "provision of corrective feedback", "number of test repetitions", administration location and timepoint, and treatment duration. (Abstract-level; moderator coefficients not retrieved.) (2nd) — [Yang et al. 2021 record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- [E] Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017) meta-analysis, as summarised: practice tests vs restudy had a moderately large positive effect; effects appeared in lab and classroom; were "stronger when the practice test and the final test had the same format"; feedback added a slightly larger, non-significant increment. (2nd) — [TIE Online summary](https://www.tieonline.com/article/2232/the-benefits-of-practice-testing)
- [E] Classroom retrieval practice in secondary-school studies "typically improved unit and end of semester examination scores by a full letter grade (approximately 10%)". — [Carpenter, Pan & Butler 2022 PDF](https://www.teachertoolkit.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/s44159-022-00089-1.pdf)
- [E, observational] Osterhage (2021), *J Microbiol Biol Educ* 22:e00139-21, intro biology, n = 341: one voluntary online practice test before each of four exams, 45-60 multiple-choice questions drawn from previous exams, "timed to match each exam (75 min for tests 1 to 3, 120 min for the final)", unproctored, correct answers shown after submission. Takers vs non-takers: exam 1 78.0% vs 71.4% (+6.6); exam 2 76.7% vs 72.7% (+4.0, n.s.); exam 3 80.6% vs 76.3% (+4.3); final 82.0% vs 77.6% (+4.4). Effect size 0.48 on exam 1, mean 0.37. Uptake 81.5%, 87.2%, 83.2% for the midterms and 54.6% for the final. — [Osterhage 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8442020/)
- [E, observational] Calibration in the same study (predicted minus actual score): on exam 1 non-takers over-predicted by 10.0 points vs 6.6 for takers (effect size 0.31, significant); no significant difference on exams 2-4. In the lowest quartile, practice-test completers trended toward more miscalibration; in the top quartile, completion was associated with underestimation (P = 0.05, effect size -0.34). Conclusion: "many of the lowest-performing students continued to significantly overestimate their knowledge ... despite feedback from practice tests". How far before the exam students sat the practice test was not tracked. — [Osterhage 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8442020/)
- [E, correlational] Naujoks, Harder & Händel (2022): two undergraduate educational-psychology courses, four voluntary practice tests across the term, without feedback (Study 1, N = 201) or with individual corrective feedback (Study 2, N = 111). "A higher number of taken practice tests was related to higher final course exam performance" and to two measures of metacognitive judgment accuracy (absolute accuracy and sensitivity). (2nd, abstract) — [Naujoks et al. 2022, "Testing pays off twice"](https://open.fau.de/handle/openfau/33034)
- [E, correlational] Walck-Shannon et al. (2021): "completed old exams" predicted exam 2 score (b = 5.85 points, p <= 0.001) after controls; completing problem sets (b = 4.32 and 7.68), self-quizzing (b = 4.38 and 5.40) and explaining concepts (b = 5.54 and 6.29) predicted both exams. Rewriting notes (b = -4.18, exam 2) and re-watching lecture (b = -2.66, exam 2) were negatively associated. — [Walck-Shannon et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8108503/)
- [E, correlational] Why students self-test: 54% "to figure out how well I have learned the information", 27% because "I learn more that way than I would through rereading", 9% "usually do not quiz myself". 71% regularly "test yourself with questions or practice problems"; almost all students with GPA > 3.6 reported doing so. — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- [E] Persistence of overconfidence: Hacker et al. (2000), Miller & Geraci (2011) and Foster et al. (2017), as cited in later work: poor performers over-predict exam scores, "sometimes up to 30%"; incentives alone did not improve calibration; with incentives plus detailed feedback low performers improved from exam 1 to exam 2 but not afterwards; grade feedback alone did not improve calibration across many exams. (2nd) — [Osterhage 2021 (PMC), literature review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8442020/)
- [E, quasi-experimental] Medical students: those below the median on weekly formative assessments benefited from an extra cumulative practice exam; those above did not need it. (2nd) — [Chang & Wimmers 2017 record](https://doaj.org/article/0265496ed7b240b3a398ffcd66cbbf45)
- [C] Cornell's plan puts a 1-hour self-test covering all chunks on the last study day, the day before the exam, and defines "review" as "blank page testing, quizzing yourself or a friend, and taking practice exams". — [Cornell LSC five-day study plan](https://lsc.cornell.edu/how-to-study/studying-for-and-taking-exams/the-five-day-study-plan/)

### Inferences
- Evidence supports these mock-exam properties: same question format as the real exam, same time limit, corrective feedback immediately after, and a follow-up session on the missed items. "Closed-book" is implied by the retrieval-practice mechanism but was not isolated in any study found.
- A mock exam does double duty: it is a retrieval session and the best available calibration signal. Because low scorers stay overconfident even after seeing their score, the software should convert the mock result into schedule changes automatically (re-weight weak topics, add sessions) and not rely on the student to self-correct.
- Because retrieval benefits need a later session to repair errors, the mock should sit early enough to leave at least one or two repair sessions: about 3-5 days before the exam, with a shorter second self-test on the last day. This timing is an inference; no study found tests it.
- Uptake of the cumulative practice test dropped to 55% before the final in Osterhage's data, exactly when it covered the most material, so the product should schedule the final-exam mock explicitly.

### Gaps
- No experiment found on the optimal number of full practice exams or days-before-exam placement.
- No study found that isolates timed vs untimed or closed- vs open-book practice exams on real course exam scores.
- Moderator values in Yang et al. (2021) (feedback, number of repetitions) were not retrieved; a blog figure of g = 0.73 with feedback vs 0.39 without belongs to Rowland (2014), a different, lab-based meta-analysis, and was not verified.

## 3. Post-lecture review: how soon the first review should happen; forgetting of lecture content; same-day free recall

### Takeaway
The tested course protocols start retrieval practice on the day the lecture introduces the material and repeat it on separate days; that produced the exam and retention gains in section 1. There is no clean, modern measurement of how much lecture content is lost after 24 hours, and "review within 24 hours" as a specific threshold is convention. A short same-day free-recall ("brain dump") is consistent with retrieval-practice evidence, but the first retrieval must be followed by feedback and by later spaced sessions; one same-day pass alone is the "too short gap" case.

### Cited Findings
- [E] In the classroom successive-relearning studies, "students began using successive relearning to learn a set of concepts when the instructor introduced them in class", then relearned on three or more later sessions at least one day apart. — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] Higham et al. (2022): practice began "after each lecture of a large" course and ran on three separate days; retrieval-based practice beat time-matched restudy at 42 and 68 days. — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] A zero-day gap is the worst schedule: with study time equal, the optimal gap gave 10% better recall than a zero gap at a 7-day test and 59% better at a 35-day test; for a 7-day test the best gap tested was 1 day. — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [E] Testing during the lecture itself: Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013), *PNAS*: interpolated memory tests during a 21-minute online statistics lecture reduced mind wandering and improved learning; author summary: untested students reported mind wandering about 40% of the time, tested students were "half as likely to mind wander", "three times as likely to take additional notes". (Numbers from the author interview; paper figures not retrieved.) (2nd) — [Szpunar et al. 2013 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/); [author interview](https://medicalresearch.com/interpolated-memory-tests-reduce-mind-wandering-and-improve-learning-of-online-lectures/)
- [E] Forgetting-curve replication: Murre & Dros (2015), *PLOS ONE*: one subject, 70 hours of learning and relearning nonsense-syllable lists at 20 min, 1 h, 9 h, 1 day, 2 days, 31 days; the Ebbinghaus curve was replicated, "not completely smooth but most probably shows a jump upwards starting at the 24 hour data point". Measures are savings scores for meaningless material, not percent of lecture content retained. — [Murre & Dros 2015 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/plo/pone00/0120644.html)
- [E, weak/old] Immediately after a lecture, students retained roughly 40-46% of content in one older study, and "not more than 42%" in another, both measured right after class. (2nd; original studies not retrieved) — [Faculty Focus](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/instructional-design/student-attention-spans); [NCSU course page](https://faculty.chass.ncsu.edu/slatta/hi216/nolecture.htm)
- [E, null result] A 2019 pharmacy-education study found "no difference in performance ... between students who restudied the recorded lecture versus students who retrieved information about the lecture on a quiz one week later". (2nd) — [PMC6920633](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6920633)
- [E, correlational] Re-watching lecture as an exam-preparation strategy was negatively associated with exam score (b = -2.66, exam 2). — [Walck-Shannon et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8108503/)
- [C] Practitioner description of the "brain dump": pause and have students "write down everything they can remember", keep it no-stakes; to add spacing, "ask students to write down what they can remember from yesterday". — [RetrievalPractice.org, brain dump](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/2017/free-recall)

### Inferences
- Defensible rule: schedule a first retrieval attempt on lecture day (after class, not as a replacement for the later sessions), then a second on a different day 1-3 days later. Lecture-day retrieval is the "initial learning to criterion" step in the tested protocols; the benefit in those studies came from the whole sequence.
- A 5-10 minute free-recall dump followed by checking notes fits the mechanism (retrieval + feedback) and the measured time costs (first learning of 8-10 definitions takes 30-40 minutes, so a 5-minute dump is a partial first pass, not the full session).
- "Within 24 hours" should be shown to users as a convenience default, not as an evidence-based cutoff. The evidence says: retrieve at least once before much is lost, and do not do all repetitions on the same day.
- A next-day recall of yesterday's lecture ("what do I remember from yesterday?") is a cheap way to implement the 1-day gap that was optimal for a 1-week horizon in Cepeda et al.

### Gaps
- No modern study found that measures percent of lecture content retained at 24 hours and 1 week with and without a same-day review. Widely shared "you forget 50-80% in 24 hours" figures for lectures are not traceable to a primary lecture study.
- No experiment found comparing first review at 0 h vs 24 h vs 48 h after a lecture with later sessions held constant.
- No direct test of a post-class free-recall "brain dump" on course exam scores was retrieved; Lyle & Crawford (2011), who tested end-of-class retrieval in a statistics course, was sought but not found in the searches.

## 4. Pre-lecture preparation: pretesting / prequestions, reading before lecture, and how much time it is worth

### Takeaway
Attempting questions before instruction reliably improves later memory for the questioned content (g = 0.54 across 97 effect sizes) but gives essentially no benefit for the rest of the lecture (g = 0.04 across 91), and works better for facts than concepts. It is therefore worth a few minutes on a handful of questions that target the lecture's key points, not a long preparation block. No quantitative evidence was found on how many minutes of pre-reading are worth scheduling.

### Cited Findings
- [E] St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2024), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 31, 411-441, preregistered meta-analysis: "a moderate specific effect (g = 0.54, k = 97) but a virtually nonexistent general effect (g = 0.04, k = 91)". General effect CI [-0.04, 0.11]; "54% of studies showed positive effects and 46% ... either no effect or a negative effect". For the specific effect 85% of effect sizes were positive. Bias-adjusted specific effect g = 0.45. — [St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024 (NSF PAR PDF)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- [E] Moderators: specific effect larger for factual prequestions (g = 0.58) than conceptual (g = 0.28), p = .073; adults g = 0.62. The general (untested-material) effect was larger for audiovisual materials (g = 0.17) than text (g = -0.005), p = .017; for massed study (g = 0.11) than interleaved (g = -0.07); and when the untested final questions were related to the prequestions (g = 0.17 vs 0.01). Both effects were larger with experimenter-paced than self-paced study. — [St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- [E] Typical designs: "they might answer 10 prequestions and then read an essay", or "two prequestions before each of five short study segments" (Carpenter & Toftness 2017, video lecture). — [St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- [E] Timing gap is untested: "only two known studies have manipulated the delay between the pre-study and study phases", so the meta-analysis dropped that variable. Classroom translation is flagged by the authors as still to be established. — [St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- [E, review] Pan & Carpenter (2023), "Prequestioning and pretesting effects: a review of empirical research, theoretical perspectives, and implications for educational practice", *Educational Psychology Review* 35(4), article 97. Lead author's summary: "pretesting can benefit learning if there is an opportunity to study the correct answers afterward". Secondary summaries add that benefits may be more pronounced at longer delays and that students should be told why they are being asked questions they cannot yet answer. (Full text not accessible; publisher redirect.) (2nd) — [Pan & Carpenter 2023 (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-023-09814-5); [TechLearning interview with Pan](https://www.techlearning.com/news/the-power-of-pretesting-why-and-how-to-implement-low-stakes-tests); [Lovell summary](https://www.ollielovell.com/how-you-can-use-prequestioning-to-boost-student-learning/)
- [E] Pretesting before a lecture video reduced mind wandering and improved final test performance relative to a control activity. (2nd, from search summary of a 2024 *Psychological Research* paper) — [Springer, Psychological Research 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-024-02042-8)
- [Critique] Most pretesting studies compare prequestions with no activity, not with another active use of the same minutes. — [Education Rickshaw commentary](https://educationrickshaw.com/why-im-skeptical-about-pre-testing/)

### Inferences
- Budget small: 3-5 minutes, about 5-10 short prequestions (or 2 per lecture segment) drawn from the lecture's stated objectives or slide headings, answered by guessing, immediately before or on the day of the lecture. The lecture itself supplies the feedback, which the review literature treats as necessary.
- Because the benefit is specific to the questioned content, choose prequestions that cover the exam-relevant core; do not expect them to lift the whole lecture.
- Prequestions are better suited to factual/terminology-heavy lectures than to conceptual or problem-solving ones (g = 0.58 vs 0.28).
- Reuse the same prequestions as the post-lecture retrieval questions: the specific effect then compounds with retrieval practice, and the student sees the before/after.

### Gaps
- No quantitative evidence retrieved on reading the textbook or slides before lecture (minutes vs exam gain). It should be presented as optional convention.
- Optimal delay between prequestions and the lecture is unknown (k = 2 studies).
- The Pan & Carpenter (2023) review text, including any explicit recommendations and effect sizes, was not read directly. A reported d = 0.30 advantage of pretesting over post-testing (Pan & Sana 2021) appeared only in a search summary and is unverified.

## 5. Session structure: session length, breaks, time of day, and daily maximum of effective study

### Takeaway
Evidence on breaks is small and mixed: one field experiment (n = 87) found pre-set breaks (24/6 or 12/3 minutes) gave less fatigue and distraction and the same task completion in less time than self-chosen breaks, while a 2025 follow-up (n = 94) found no differences between Pomodoro 25/5, Flowtime and self-regulated breaks over a 2-hour session. No study establishes an optimal session length or a daily cap; the "about 4 hours of deliberate practice" figure is a loosely sourced upper bound. Time-of-day evidence favours earlier-in-the-day and non-late-night study only correlationally.

### Cited Findings
- [E] Biwer, Wiradhany, oude Egbrink & de Bruin (2023), *British Journal of Educational Psychology* 93(S2), 353-367: 87 Dutch university students, one day of real self-study. Conditions: self-regulated breaks (n = 35); systematic-long "Pomodoro", "a 6-min break after every 24-min study block" (n = 25); systematic-short, "a 3-min break after every 12-min study block" (n = 27). Self-regulating students "had longer study sessions and breaks", with "higher levels of fatigue and distractedness, and lower levels of concentration and motivation". "No difference between groups in invested mental effort or task completion." Authors: systematic breaks "had mood benefits and appeared to have efficiency benefits (i.e., similar task completion in shorter time)". — [Biwer et al. 2023 (Maastricht record)](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/understanding-effort-regulation-comparing-pomodoro-breaks-and-sel); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/)
- [E] Smits, Wenzel & de Bruin (2025), *Behavioral Sciences* 15(7), 861: 94 Dutch psychology students, 2-hour online study session. Self-regulated (n = 25); Pomodoro, four 25-min blocks each followed by a 5-min break (n = 36); Flowtime, study as long as focused then break 5 min (block <= 25 min), 8 min (25-50 min) or 10 min (> 50 min) (n = 33). Breaks taken: 3.04, 4.00, 2.82; total break time 0.30 h, 0.34 h, 0.30 h. No group differences in task completion (70.3%, 69.4%, 67.1%; p = 0.854), productivity, or flow (4.22, 4.10, 3.98 on 1-7). Fatigue rose faster in Pomodoro (p = 0.025); motivation fell faster in Pomodoro (p = 0.008) and Flowtime (p = 0.039) than self-regulated, without overall level differences. Authors: "no one of these break-taking techniques should be more highly recommended over the others." — [Smits et al. 2025 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)
- [E] Self-regulating students in Smits et al. chose on their own about 3 breaks totalling about 18 minutes in 2 hours, i.e. roughly 1.7 h of study per 2 h, the same ratio as Pomodoro. — [Smits et al. 2025 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292963/)
- [E, review] A 2025 *BMC Medical Education* scoping review (32 studies, total N = 5,270, three RCTs) reports that across the RCTs structured intervals (24/6, 12/3) gave "approximately 20% lower fatigue compared with self-paced breaks". The RCT subsample appears to be the Biwer data; treat as the same evidence, not independent confirmation. (2nd) — [BMC Medical Education 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12909-025-08001-0)
- [E, lab] Ariga & Lleras (2011), *Cognition* 118(3), 439-443: 84 participants on a roughly 50-minute vigilance task (one summary says 40 minutes). Performance "steeply declined over time" without interruption; when participants briefly switched to a digit-memory probe twice during the task, "the vigilance decrement was averted". Mechanism proposed: goal habituation, not resource depletion. The task was a repetitive line-detection task, not studying. (2nd for numbers) — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/); [University of Illinois news](https://news.illinois.edu/brief-diversions-vastly-improve-focus-researchers-find/)
- [E, observational, schoolchildren] Sievertsen, Gino & Piovesan (2016), *PNAS* 113(10), 2621-2624, all Danish public-school pupils 2009/10-2012/13: "for every hour later in the day, test performance decreases by 0.9% of a standard deviation (95% CI: 0.7%-1.0%)"; "a 20-30 minute break improves average test performance by 1.7% of a standard deviation (95% CI: 1.2%-2.2%)". — [Sievertsen et al. 2016 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4790980)
- [E, correlational] When students study: evening 69%, late night 20%, afternoon 11%, morning < 1%; yet 42% believed morning or afternoon study would be most effective. "The lowest performers are most likely to engage in late-night studying." — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- [E, correlational] Distraction: students reported being distracted for a mean 20.7% (SD 16.5) of exam-study time; 61% were distracted for more than 10% of it; each 10 points of distraction went with about 1 point lower exam score (b = -0.093 and -0.119 per percentage point). — [Walck-Shannon et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8108503/)
- [E, correlational] Chronotype: across 40,890 grades in a Dutch secondary school, late chronotypes scored lower, mainly in science subjects and in early-day exams; the earliest vs latest 20% of chronotypes differed by about the 55th vs 43rd percentile. A 2023 study found a synchrony effect for crystallized but not fluid intelligence tests in morning classes. Secondary-school data, not university. — [van der Vinne/Zerbini et al. (PMC5491513)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5491513/); [J. Intelligence 2023 (PMC9862939)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9862939/)
- [E, weak / C] Daily ceiling: Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) reported that older training studies show "essentially no benefit from durations exceeding 4 hr per day and reduced benefits from practice exceeding 2 hr", and that lab studies of extended practice limit it to "about 1 hr for 3-5 days a week". A critical spot-check argues the evidence behind that paragraph is thin and the 4-hour figure is often wrongly attributed to the violinist data. (2nd) — [Epistemic spot check of Ericsson et al. 1993](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/8EmPhoxgSXGSdq4Cb/epistemic-spot-check-the-role-of-deliberate-practice-in-the)
- [C] Learning-centre session length: "30 to 60-minute study sessions" on "a little bit of material at a time"; Cornell's plan uses about 2 hours per day per exam. — [University of Iowa, Top 5 Effective Study Strategies](https://learning.uiowa.edu/sites/learning.uiowa.edu/files/2025-04/Top%205%20Effective%20Study%20Strategies.pdf); [Cornell LSC](https://lsc.cornell.edu/how-to-study/studying-for-and-taking-exams/the-five-day-study-plan/)

### Inferences
- Any regular break scheme is defensible; none is proven superior for output. Offer 25/5 (or 24/6) as a default because it removes the decision and, in one study, reduced fatigue and overrun, but let users switch to self-paced without penalty. Do not claim Pomodoro increases learning.
- Blocks of 25-50 minutes with 5-10 minute breaks cover every tested configuration (12/3, 24/6, 25/5, Flowtime up to 50+/10). Nothing tested supports unbroken blocks beyond about 50 minutes.
- Measured time costs (section 1) suggest natural unit sizes: 30-40 minutes for a first retrieval-to-criterion pass on one lecture's 8-10 key concepts; 15 minutes for the first relearning; 5 minutes for later ones. A scheduler can therefore pack several short relearning items into one 25-minute block.
- A daily cap is a product decision, not a research finding. If one is needed, about 4 hours of high-effort study per day (beyond lectures) is the commonly cited upper bound, and it should be labelled as convention.
- Schedule study blocks before late evening by default: late-night study is the pattern most associated with low GPA, and it conflicts with the sleep findings in section 6. This is correlational.
- A "distraction-free" flag for sessions is supported by field data of about the same size as one extra active strategy (roughly 1 exam point per 10 points of distraction).

### Gaps
- No experiment found that varies study-session length (e.g. 25 vs 50 vs 90 minutes) and measures learning or exam results.
- Both Dutch break studies measured mood and task completion, not learning or retention.
- No university-level experimental evidence on time of day of study and exam performance.
- The primary Ericsson (1993) text and Ariga & Lleras full results were not read directly.

## 6. Sleep: consolidation, the cost of all-nighters, and reviewing before sleep

### Takeaway
Across a term, sleep regularity, duration and quality over the preceding week and month correlate with grades (sleep measures explained nearly 25% of variance in one course; regularity r = 0.37 with GPA), whereas the single night before a test did not predict the score. Averaging 8 hours for the five nights before finals was associated with about 4 points higher on the exam. Sleep restriction specifically damages massed (crammed) material and spares spaced material. Vocabulary learned shortly before sleep is retained better than vocabulary followed by a waking day, in small lab studies.

### Cited Findings
- [E, correlational] Okano, Kaczmarzyk, Dave, Gabrieli & Grossman (2019), *npj Science of Learning*: 100 students in an MIT introductory chemistry class wore activity trackers (88 completed). "Better quality, longer duration, and greater consistency of sleep correlated with better grades"; sleep measures "accounted for nearly 25% of the variance in academic performance". "Sleep measures from the single night before a test showed no relation to test performance"; sleep over the week and month before did. Bedtime after about 2 a.m. (median bedtime 1:47 a.m. in one report) was associated with lower scores regardless of total sleep. (Abstract and press coverage.) (2nd) — [Okano et al. 2019 (MIT DSpace)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/127257); [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/sleep-college-grades-15007/)
- [E, correlational] Phillips et al. (2017), *Scientific Reports* 7:3216: 61 Harvard undergraduates, 30 days of sleep diaries; the Sleep Regularity Index correlated with academic performance (r = 0.37, p < 0.004). Regular and irregular sleepers did not differ in average sleep duration. Irregular sleepers had later circadian timing: melatonin onset 00:08 vs 21:32. — [Phillips et al. 2017 (MIT DSpace)](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/113615); [Brigham and Women's press release](https://www.brighamandwomens.org/about-bwh/newsroom/press-releases-detail?id=2742)
- [E, incentive field study] Scullin (2019; online December 2018), "The 8-Hour Sleep Challenge During Final Exams Week", *Teaching of Psychology*: students offered extra credit for averaging 8 hours of sleep over the five nights before the final, verified by wrist actigraphy. Meeting the challenge "was associated with a four-point grade boost, even prior to applying extra credit", after adjusting for prior course grade. Small, self-selected groups (e.g. 8 of 18 psychology students opted in). (2nd) — [Neuroscience News coverage](https://neurosciencenews.com/8-hour-sleep-students-120197/); [Baylor coverage](https://www2.baylor.edu/baylorproud/?p=21728)
- [E, randomized] Huang et al. (2016), *Sleep*, "Sleep restriction impairs vocabulary learning when adolescents cram for exams: the Need for Sleep Study": 56 adolescents aged 15-19 randomly assigned to a week of 5 h or 9 h time in bed. Word pairs were studied either over 4 consecutive days (spaced) or in single sessions (massed), study time equal; recall tested at 0 h, 24 h and 120 h. Significant spacing x sleep interaction (LR chi-square = 8.40, P < 0.01): "recall of massed items was impaired by a greater amount in adolescents exposed to sleep restriction", while "performance on spaced items was similar between sleep groups". Authors: "spaced learning conferred strong protection against the effects of sleep restriction". — [Huang et al. 2016 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989257)
- [E, lab, small] Gais, Lucas & Born (2006), *Learning & Memory* 13(3), 259-262: 12 high-school students learned English-German vocabulary; with retention intervals of 24 h and 36 h and morning vs evening learning crossed, "declarative memory is enhanced when sleep follows within a few hours of learning, independent of time of day". Sleep deprivation after learning hurt memory, still measurable after a recovery night. — [Gais et al. 2006 abstract](https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/13/3/259.abstract)
- [E, review] Diekelmann & Born (2010), "The memory function of sleep", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 11, 114-126: sleep optimizes consolidation of newly acquired information "depending on the specific conditions of learning and the timing of sleep". A companion review reports "declarative memory profits already from rather short sleep periods (1-2 h)" and that a short delay between learning and sleep optimizes the benefit. (Abstract-level.) (2nd) — [Diekelmann, Wilhelm & Born, "The whats and whens of sleep-dependent memory consolidation"](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/the-whats-and-whens-of-sleep-dependent-memory-consolidation/)
- [E, lab] Sleep before learning: Yoo, Hu, Gujar, Jolesz & Walker (2007), *Nature Neuroscience* 10(3), 385-392, "A deficit in the ability to form new human memories without sleep". Walker describes the result as "almost a 40% deficit in the ability of the brain to make new memories" after a night of total sleep deprivation. The 40% figure comes from Walker's lecture, not checked against the paper. (2nd) — [Walker lecture summary](https://www.masterclass.com/classes/matthew-walker-teaches-the-science-of-better-sleep/chapters/fire-up-the-brain)
- [E, correlational, self-report] Thacher (2008): of 111 students, two-thirds had pulled at least one all-nighter in a semester; those who did so regularly had lower GPAs. (2nd) — [The Ubyssey summary](https://ubyssey.ca/science/studyology-all-nighter)
- [E, descriptive] During exam periods sleep duration fell by up to several hours and more than 10% of students did not sleep at all the night before (one study, as summarised in search results; primary source not identified). (2nd) — [The Ubyssey summary](https://ubyssey.ca/science/studyology-all-nighter)

### Inferences
- The schedule should protect sleep across the whole exam week (target about 8 h, consistent bed and wake times), not only the night before: the measured associations are with the prior week/month and with regularity.
- Hard constraints that follow: no study blocks scheduled past a fixed cutoff (the data point to trouble after about 1-2 a.m.; a product default of ending by 23:00 is a conservative convention), and the plan should never propose an all-nighter. If a student is behind, cut scope by priority instead of extending into the night.
- Because sleep loss hurts crammed material and spares spaced material, the spacing schedule is also the mitigation for students who sleep badly in exam week.
- A short evening retrieval session for declarative material (definitions, vocabulary, formulas) placed 1-3 hours before bed is supported by small lab studies; it should be brief and must not push bedtime later.
- An early-morning exam after a late study night combines three risks found here: late bedtime, irregularity, and (for late chronotypes) testing at an early internal time.

### Gaps
- No randomized study in university students of all-nighter vs normal sleep before a real course exam was found; Thacher (2008) is self-report and correlational, and Okano et al. found no single-night effect.
- Scullin's 8-hour challenge had small self-selected samples; exact n and statistics were not retrieved.
- No classroom test of "review before sleep" vs the same review earlier in the day on course exam scores.
- Walker/Stickgold primary reviews and the Diekelmann & Born (2010) full text were not read directly.

## 7. Workload norms: credit-hour conventions, what students actually do, realistic daily minute budgets

### Takeaway
The regulatory norms (US: at least 2 hours of out-of-class work per credit hour per week; ECTS: 25-30 hours of total workload per credit, 1,500-1,800 hours per year) are definitions of intended workload, not evidence about learning. US students report about 13-15 hours per week preparing for class in total, roughly half the norm for a 15-credit load. A realistic plan budget is therefore closer to 25-35 minutes per course per day than the 70-85 minutes the norm implies.

### Cited Findings
- [C, regulation] US federal credit hour (34 CFR 600.2): an amount of work that reasonably approximates not less than "one hour of classroom or direct faculty instruction and a minimum of two hours of out-of-class student work each week for approximately fifteen weeks for one semester or trimester hour of credit, or ten to twelve weeks for one quarter hour". Common practice counts the class hour as 50 minutes. — [University of Cincinnati, credit hour definition](https://uc.edu/about/registrar/registration/policies/credit-hour-definition.html)
- [C] "Two hours of study time for every hour you are in class" is the usual learning-centre restatement; another guide says "an average course requires around 10 hours of your time per week". — [Ohio University time management](https://www.ohio.edu/cas/undergraduate/success-support/time-management)
- [C, guidance] ECTS Users' Guide 2015: 60 ECTS credits correspond to the workload of a full-time academic year; "in most cases, workload ranges from 1,500 to 1,800 hours for an academic year, which means that one credit corresponds to 25 to 30 hours of work". Workload includes contact hours, self-study and exams. The guide is non-normative; hours per credit are set nationally (the UK equates a credit with about 20 hours). — [HEAd'22 paper quoting the guide](https://archive.headconf.org/head22/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/14231.pdf); [BFUG-endorsed draft of the 2015 guide](https://pjp-eu.coe.int/bih-higher-education/images/4.%20the%20bologna%20follow-up%20group%20(bfug)%20endorsed%20new%20(2015)%20ects%20users%27%20guide.pdf)
- [Critique] A 2022 conference paper argues the 25-30 h/credit equivalence is "oversized", harms education quality and student health, and proposes 20 hours per credit. — [HEAd'22 paper](https://archive.headconf.org/head22/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/14231.pdf)
- [E, survey] NSSE 2024, as reported: first-year students estimated "just 6.3 hours a week doing assigned reading" and "a total of 14.3 hours preparing for class". (2nd; think-tank article citing NSSE) — [Manhattan Institute](https://manhattan.institute/article/what-do-college-students-do-all-day-the-answer-isnt-studying)
- [E, survey] NSSE 2013 Annual Results: "First-year students spent an average of 14 hours per week preparing for class, and seniors averaged one hour more. Of this, six and seven hours per week, respectively, were devoted to assigned reading." — [NSSE Annual Results 2013 (IU ScholarWorks)](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/items/8866215a-6ea9-4dee-b051-1aa72b413025/full)
- [E, survey] Institution-level NSSE figures cluster at 13-16 h/week: SUNY Oswego first-years 15.6 h vs 13.3 h (SUNY peers) and 13.5 h (public comprehensives), 2023; Western Carolina 13 h (first-year) and 14 h (senior), 2025; Framingham State 13 h and 13 h, 2022; at Nicholls (2014) 32% of first-years and 38% of seniors spent more than 15 h/week. — [SUNY Oswego NSSE 2023 presentation](https://ww1.oswego.edu/faculty-assembly/sites/faculty-assembly/files/nsse_2023_presentation_to_december_faculty_assembly.pdf); [WCU NSSE 2025 pocket guide](https://www-dr.wcupa.edu/deputy-provost/institutionalResearch/documents/NSSE25%20Pocket%20Guide%20Report%20WUC_web.pdf); [Framingham NSSE 2022](https://www.Framingham.Edu/Assets/uploads/about-fsu/office-of-institutional-research/_documents/NSSE/NSSE22%20Pocket%20Guide%20Report%20(FSU).pdf); [Nicholls NSSE 2012/2014](https://www.nicholls.edu/irep/national-survey-of-student-engagement-2012-administration/)
- [E, course-level] In one intro biology course, students reported 3.33-3.57 study hours per week for that course. — [Bailey et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8062001)
- [E, course-level] How that time is used: a mean 52-55% of exam-study time was spent on active strategies (SD 24 points), students used a mean 4.2 active strategies, and each additional active strategy went with 1.9% and 2.8% higher exam scores; 10 more percentage points of active time went with about 0.55 and 1.0 points. — [Walck-Shannon et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8108503/)
- [C] Exam-specific budgets: Cornell's five-day plan totals 10 h 45 min per exam; adapted handouts say 8-10 hours may be needed for an A or B. — [Cornell LSC](https://lsc.cornell.edu/how-to-study/studying-for-and-taking-exams/the-five-day-study-plan/)

### Inferences
(Arithmetic on the cited norms; assumptions stated.)
- US norm, 3-credit course: 6 h/week out of class = about 51 min/day over 7 days or 72 min/day over 5 days. Five such courses = 30 h/week = about 4.3 h/day over 7 days.
- US actual (about 14 h/week total, assume 5 courses): about 2.8 h/week per course = about 24 min/day over 7 days or 34 min/day over 5 days. Students do roughly 45-50% of the norm.
- ECTS, 30-credit semester: 750-900 h total. Over an assumed 20 weeks including the exam period that is 37.5-45 h/week including contact time. A 5-ECTS course = 125-150 h; with an assumed 3-4 contact hours per week over 14 weeks (42-56 h), self-study is about 70-108 h, i.e. about 3.5-5.5 h/week or 30-47 min/day over 7 days across 20 weeks.
- Suggested product defaults: baseline 30 min/day per course on non-exam weeks (matches observed behaviour, about half the norm), "norm" mode 50-60 min/day per course, and an exam ramp adding about 10 hours per exam spread over the final 5-7 days (about 90-120 min/day for that course). These are conventions derived from the figures above.
- The more useful lever than total minutes is the share of minutes that is retrieval-based: field data show an hour of study is worth about 0.13-0.20 exam points, whereas adopting one more active strategy is worth about 2-3 points.

### Gaps
- The official NSSE national frequency tables for 2023-2025 were not retrieved directly; the 2024 figure comes through a secondary article. No full-time-only breakdown found.
- The final published ECTS Users' Guide 2015 PDF was not opened; wording is quoted via a paper and a pre-approval draft.
- No European equivalent of NSSE time-use data (e.g. EUROSTUDENT) was searched.
- No evidence found on a minimum effective daily dose per course.

## 8. Cumulative review: keeping earlier lectures alive, and weighting topics by exam relevance and by weakness

### Takeaway
Cumulative testing improves retention after the course, with the benefit concentrated in weaker students and introductory courses; high performers gain little, and one biology study found no average effect. For a planner this supports reserving a slice of every week for older lectures, sized larger for students with lower scores. Weighting by personal weakness is supported indirectly (successive relearning drops items once recalled correctly and repeats missed ones); weighting by exam relevance is convention.

### Cited Findings
- [E, quasi-experimental] Khanna, Badura Brack & Finken (2013), "Short- and Long-Term Effects of Cumulative Finals on Student Learning", *Teaching of Psychology* 40: sections with cumulative finals had higher content-exam scores immediately after the final, and students "retained more than students who took noncumulative finals" up to 18 months later; "introductory psychology students benefited more from cumulative finals than did upper-division course students". Even in the best condition students answered only 82% of content items; at 18 months in introductory courses with non-cumulative finals they retained "a little over half". (2nd) — [Khanna et al. 2013 record](https://researchworks.creighton.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Short--and-Long-Term-Effects-of-Cumulative/991005931127602656); [MRU SoTL summary](https://blogs.mtroyal.ca/isotl/2013/08/06/short-and-long-term-effects-of-cumulative-finals-on-student-learning/)
- [E, quasi-experimental] Lawrence (2013), "Cumulative Exams in the Introductory Psychology Course", *Teaching of Psychology* 40, 15-19: one section took three 50-question exams where exams 2 and 3 each included 10 questions on earlier units; the other took non-cumulative exams; both took the same final (55% cumulative). Differences on the final, chapter quizzes and course grade favoured the cumulative section. On a 50-question follow-up two months after the course, "high scorers' retention was not affected", "low-scoring students remembered more" with cumulative exams. Students liked the cumulative format less. (2nd) — [Faculty Focus summary](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/educational-assessment/examining-the-benefits-of-cumulative-tests-and-finals/)
- [E, quasi-experimental, null on average] Bailey, Greenall, Tullis & Williams (2021), *PLoS One* 16(4):e0250143: intro biology, seven cumulative vs seven non-cumulative 12-item midterms. No effect on the final exam (F(1,342) = 0.27, p = 0.61) or on a retention exam about five months later (t(60) = 1.16, p = 0.25; only 62 returned). Interaction with reasoning skill (p = 0.003): cumulative midterms were estimated to raise retention about 15% for the lowest reasoners and lower it about 15% for the highest. Students in the cumulative section were more likely to review a past midterm before the next one (p = 0.037). The paper cites a medical-school study with 69 more self-reported study hours under cumulative testing (Kerdijk et al. 2015). — [Bailey et al. 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8062001)
- [E] Material relearned across sessions stays available: students who used successive relearning "will retain much of the material across the course (Rawson et al., 2013), and for material they have forgotten, relearning will be rapid", so boosters before a cumulative final "should not take a great deal of time"; measured relearning times fall from about 15 minutes to under 5 minutes per 8-10-concept set. — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf)
- [E] Weakness-weighting is built into the tested protocol: within a session "if correct, the item was dropped from that session; if the answer was incorrect, the students continued to practice it more". When students instead controlled the flashcards themselves (e.g. deciding when to drop cards), retrieval gave no exam benefit in a related study. (second sentence 2nd) — [Dunlosky et al. chapter](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-successive-relearning-dunlosky-greve-badali-wissman-rawson.pdf); [RetrievalPractice.org, successive relearning](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning)
- [E, correlational] What students do unaided: when convinced they know an answer, 54% "put it aside and focus on other material" and 46% "make sure to study (or test yourself on) it again later"; 24% choose what to study by "whatever I feel I'm doing the worst in"; only 2% by "whatever I haven't studied for the longest time"; 78% do not return to material after the course ends. — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- [E] Self-judged weakness is unreliable for weak students: low performers over-predict exam scores and stay overconfident after practice-test feedback. — [Osterhage 2021 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8442020/)
- [E] Long-horizon spacing: for retention over about a year the optimal review gap is about 3-4 weeks (27 days for a 350-day interval). — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [C] Exam-relevance weighting: "focus study time on your weakest areas"; weight preparation toward the exam worth more of the grade (e.g. a 25% exam over a 10% one). — [UC Berkeley College of Chemistry peer advising handout](https://chemistry.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/peer-advising-atilla-what-to-look-for-in-the-syllabus.pdf)
- [C] Cornell's plan revisits older chunks every day at shrinking doses: chunk A gets 30, 15, 15, 10 minutes on days 2-5. — [Cornell LSC](https://lsc.cornell.edu/how-to-study/studying-for-and-taking-exams/the-five-day-study-plan/)

### Inferences
- Cumulative review rule that follows: every week, reserve a fixed share of each course's time for older lectures, served as short retrieval sets. With relearning costing 5-15 minutes per lecture-sized set, 20-30% of a 30-45 minute daily budget covers one or two old lectures per day. The share is a design choice; evidence supports the direction, not the percentage.
- Old lectures should cycle on a widening but capped interval: 2 days apart in the first week after the lecture, then roughly weekly to every 2-4 weeks until the exam, with a final booster in the last 2-3 days (Cepeda gaps for 5-10 week horizons are 8-12 days).
- Prioritise by measured recall, not self-rating: sort the review queue by last retrieval result (missed first), then by time since last successful retrieval, then by exam weight. The 2% of students who choose by "not studied for longest" shows this ordering is what a tool adds over habit.
- Scale cumulative review to performance: stronger effects for low scorers suggest a larger old-material share when quiz/mock scores are low, a smaller one for consistently high scorers.
- Exam-relevance weights (points per topic, instructor emphasis, past-paper frequency) are a reasonable multiplier but should be labelled as heuristic.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests a topic-weighting algorithm (by weakness or exam weight) against equal allocation in a university course.
- Khanna et al. and Lawrence were read only via summaries; effect sizes not retrieved. All three cumulative-exam studies are quasi-experimental with section-level assignment.
- No evidence on the best share of weekly time for old vs new material.

## 9. Multiple courses and competing deadlines: evidence on splitting time between courses

### Takeaway
No study was found that tests how to divide study time among concurrent courses. What exists is descriptive: students allocate by nearest deadline, low performers most of all, and the few who plan ahead have higher GPAs. Everything prescriptive here (weight by exam value, keep touching every course during another course's exam week) is convention or inference from the spacing literature.

### Cited Findings
- [E, correlational] 56% of students decide what to study next by "whatever's due soonest/overdue"; this "is especially true for low performers"; 13% plan a schedule ahead and "high performers are more likely to do so". The UCLA sample in Kornell & Bjork (2007) gave 59% and 11%. — [Hartwig & Dunlosky 2012 PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2012-hartwig.pdf)
- [E] Logged behaviour confirms deadline-driven massing in lecture and web courses; weekly deadlines create weekly peaks. (2nd) — [Taraban, Maki & Rynearson record](https://scholars.ttu.edu/en/publications/measuring-study-time-distributions-implications-for-designing-com-5)
- [E, descriptive / hypothesis] Engineering time-on-task surveys show "very little time spent on coursework outside of class when no assignments are due and many hours ... right before a homework assignment is due or an examination is imminent"; a 2025 ASEE paper hypothesises that students rotate attention course to course so that peaks smooth out at programme level (results not retrieved). (2nd) — [ASEE 2025 paper](https://peer.asee.org/analysis-of-trends-in-student-time-on-task-across-a-program-do-apparent-peaks-and-valleys-smooth-out-when-all-program-coursework-is-considered)
- [E] Too-short gaps cost far more than too-long ones, so dropping a course for a week to cram another turns that course's reviews into late (cheap) rather than early (costly) errors only if they are resumed; skipping them entirely forfeits the spacing benefit. — [Cepeda et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- [E] Spaced material is robust to the sleep loss typical of multi-exam weeks; massed material is not. — [Huang et al. 2016 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989257)
- [C] "During the five days you are studying for your exam ... don't neglect your other courses!" — [Cornell LSC](https://lsc.cornell.edu/how-to-study/studying-for-and-taking-exams/the-five-day-study-plan/)
- [C] Expect "at least one week with two or even three exams possibly even on the same day"; weight time by each exam's share of the grade. — [UC Berkeley College of Chemistry peer advising handout](https://chemistry.berkeley.edu/sites/default/files/peer-advising-atilla-what-to-look-for-in-the-syllabus.pdf)
- [C] The credit-hour norm implies splitting time in proportion to credits (2 h out of class per credit per week). — [University of Cincinnati, credit hour definition](https://uc.edu/about/registrar/registration/policies/credit-hour-definition.html)

### Inferences
- Default allocation (convention): base share proportional to credits, multiplied by an exam-proximity ramp and by a weakness factor from recent retrieval scores; keep a floor (e.g. 10-15 minutes of retrieval) for every course on every study day so no course's spacing chain breaks during another course's exam week.
- When two exams fall in the same week, run two overlapping five-day ramps and stagger their heavy "prepare" days; the short relearning items (5-15 minutes) from the non-urgent course fit in the gaps.
- The product's core value versus student habit is replacing "due soonest" with a planned queue: the survey data show deadline-driven choice is the majority behaviour and is most common among low performers.
- Switching courses between blocks within a day is compatible with spacing (it lengthens within-course gaps); no evidence found that it harms learning, and none that it helps beyond spacing.

### Gaps
- No experimental or strong observational evidence found on time allocation across concurrent courses, on the cost of pausing one course during another's exam week, or on the best order of courses within a day.
- The interleaving literature (mixing problem types within a subject) was not searched for this note and should not be assumed to apply to mixing whole courses.
- ASEE 2025 results were not retrieved.
